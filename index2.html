Seminar 1 — Ancient Britain · Roman Britain · Anglo-Saxon England

Central concept: the formation of Britain from the first hunter-gatherers to the Norman threshold (c. 500,000 BC – 1066).

  • Ancient Britain: settlement and earliest people
    • Through long Ice Ages small bands of hunter-gatherers lived largely nomadic lives in Europe; during warmer interglacial periods some groups crossed the land bridge from the continent into what is now southern Britain.
    • The oldest pre-modern human remains found in Britain are about 500,000 years old and were discovered at Boxgrove, Sussex.
    • At Hengistbury Head in Dorset there is evidence of intermittent human habitation stretching back over 100,000 years.
    • In Kents Cavern near Torquay, Devon archaeological evidence reveals intermittent habitation in warm periods between Ice Ages; a jaw bone found there could be that of a Neanderthal Man who lived 35,000–40,000 years ago.
    • The oldest modern human remains were found at Sun Hole in the Mendips, Creswell in Derbyshire and Gough’s Cave, Cheddar — dating from about 9,000 years old (Gough’s Cave) to 12,500 years (Sun Hole).
  • Ancient Britain: climate, landscape and the Wildwood
    • About 12,000 years ago Britain’s climate began to warm dramatically; temperatures reached their peak about 6,500 years ago, and the English Channel opened up soon after this.
    • With warmer temperatures settlement became more widespread and extended into Scotland; Britain was covered in a vast forest, the Wildwood, which had slowly covered the Ice Age tundra and extended south as far as Bordeaux.
    • From 8,000 to 4,000 years ago huge areas of forest were burned off by settlers; by 500 BC half of England had been cleared; today only 1% of the Wildwood remains, and even this has been influenced by management.
    • Closest modern equivalents: forests at Glentrool (Scotland), Kingley Vale and Ebernoe Common (Sussex), Binswood (Hampshire), box woodland at Box Hill (Surrey).
    • Place-name echoes: endings -ley, -hurst, -field indicate a settlement originally in a clearing or next to a wood.
    • Before large-scale clearing people used the chalk ridges of southern England to move about; a path network grew up and a number of ancient long-distance paths survive.
  • Ancient Britain: Neolithic revolution and the search for security
    • Forest clearance was a desperate attempt to obtain land for farming; during the Ice Ages a square mile could support only about four individuals, while Neolithic farming (10,000–12,000 years ago) allowed about twenty-five people to survive on a similar area.
    • Even with this improvement there was still competition for the best land, particularly as early agricultural methods tended not to replenish the soil.
    • From about 2500 BC a period of relative prosperity for Neolithic farmers ended; archaeological investigation shows formerly cultivated land in Norfolk, Suffolk and Wessex became infested with weeds and scrub — possibly the result of soil exhaustion.
    • Two measures were taken to find security: some people fortified a hilltop with huge circular banks and ditches (outlines still visible; Maiden Castle in Dorset is one of the best examples); alternatively a representational fort was built — stylised fortifications using the same circular banks and ditches, later with standing stones.
    • Sometimes a representation of a hill itself was created, as at Silbury Hill near Avebury; in a search for spiritual security these sacred sites used all the features of defensive structures built in search of physical security.
    • They may also have had an astrological role, with certain features aligned to the sun or sometimes the moon; the two most famous structures of this kind, Stonehenge and Avebury, date to between 3000 BC and 1500 BC.
  • Ancient Britain: metals, literacy and timeline
    • As metal working improved into the Bronze Age (2100–700 BC), so did weapons; hill forts got bigger and monument building continued.
    • Around 500 BC iron became more widely used, replacing bronze; there was another leap forward in tool technology, but also another leap forward in weapons technology, with a corresponding upsurge in fear and insecurity — this led to the building of over 3,000 hill forts in the early centuries of the first millennium; Maiden Castle was extended to the huge fortification seen today.
    • Britain entered literate and recorded history in 325 BC when Mediterranean adventurers came in search of tin; Pytheas of Marseilles made the trip in 325 BC, landing in Cornwall, and wrote an account of his journey.
    • It took a visitor to put Britain on paper, since native Celts had an oral tradition and opposed writing.
    • Timeline: c. 8500 BC climate warms, forests spread, sea level rises, England cut off from Europe; c. 4500 BC farming introduced; c. 2500 BC henge monuments; c. 2000 BC bronze first used; c. 650 BC iron tools and weapons introduced by the Celts; c. 150 BC potter’s wheel introduced.
  • Roman Britain: conquest (55 BC – AD 86)
    • The written history of England really began in 55 BC when Julius Caesar led an expedition; he returned in 54 BC; both times he defeated the Celts but did not stay — the Romans withdrew after the Celts agreed to pay annual tribute.
    • The Romans invaded again in AD 43 under Emperor Claudius; they landed somewhere in south-east England and quickly prevailed against the Celtic army, which could not match the discipline and training of the Roman army.
    • A battle was fought on the River Medway, ending in Celtic defeat and withdrawal; the Romans chased them over the Thames into Essex and within months captured the Celtic hill fort on the site of Colchester.
    • Other Roman forces marched into Sussex, where the local tribe, the Atrebates, were friendly and offered no resistance; the army then marched into the territory of the Durotriges in Dorchester and southern Somerset; that year 11 Celtic kings surrendered to Claudius.
    • Normally if a Celtic king surrendered the Romans allowed him to remain as a puppet ruler; Aulus Plautius was made the first governor of Roman Britain; by AD 47 the Romans controlled England from the River Humber to the Estuary of the River Severn.
    • However the war was not over: the Silures in South Wales and the Ordovices of North Wales continued to harass the Romans, and fighting between the Welsh tribes and the Romans continued for years.
  • Roman Britain: Boudicca and the Iceni
    • Meanwhile the Iceni tribe of East Anglia rebelled; at first the Romans allowed them to keep their kings and have some autonomy.
    • However c. AD 50, while the Romans were fighting in Wales, they were afraid the Iceni might stab them in the back and ordered them to disarm, which provoked a rebellion that was easily crushed.
    • In the ensuing years the Romans alienated the Iceni by imposing heavy taxes; when the king of the Iceni died he left his kingdom partly to his wife Boudicca and partly to Emperor Nero; soon Nero wanted the kingdom all for himself, and his men treated the Iceni very high-handedly and provoked rebellion.
    • This time a large part of the Roman army was fighting in Wales, and the rebellion was at first successful: led by Boudicca the Celts burned Colchester, St Albans and London.
    • However the Romans rushed forces to deal with the rebellion; although outnumbered, their superior discipline and tactics secured total victory.
    • After the rebellion was crushed the Celts of southern and eastern England settled down and gradually accepted Roman rule.
  • Roman Britain: expansion, frontiers and decline
    • AD 71–74 the Romans conquered the north of England; 74–77 South Wales; in AD 77 Agricola was made governor of Britain — he first conquered North Wales, then turned to what is now Scotland.
    • By AD 81 the Romans had captured the area from the Clyde to the Forth; in 82 they advanced further north; in 83 they won a great victory at Mons Graupius (exact location unknown); however in 86 the Romans withdrew from Scotland.
    • In 122–126 the Emperor Hadrian built a great wall across the northern frontier to keep out the people the Romans called the Picts.
    • Under Antoninus Pius the Romans again invaded Scotland; in 142–143 they defeated the Picts and built a wall of turf with a stone base (Antonine Wall), but it was abandoned about 163 and the army withdrew to Hadrian’s Wall.
    • By the middle of the 3rd century the Roman Empire was in decline; in the latter half of the century Saxons from Germany began raiding the east coast; the Romans built a chain of forts called the Saxon Shore, commanded by an official called the Count of the Saxon Shore and containing both infantry and cavalry.
    • However the Saxon raids were at first no more than pin pricks and most of Roman Britain remained reasonably peaceful and prosperous.
    • In 286 an admiral named Carausius seized power and ruled Britain as an emperor for 7 years until Allectus, his finance minister, assassinated him; Allectus then ruled until 296 when Constantius, Emperor of the Western Roman Empire, invaded and Britain was taken back into the Roman fold.
    • In 367 Scots from Northern Ireland, Picts from Scotland and Saxons joined to raid and loot Roman Britain; they overran Hadrian’s Wall and killed the Count of the Saxon Shore; the Romans sent Theodosius with reinforcements to restore order; in 383 some Roman soldiers were withdrawn and raiding grew worse.
    • The last Roman troops left Britain in 407; in 410 the leaders of the Romano-Celts sent a letter to the Emperor Honorius appealing for help, but he had no troops to spare and told the Britons they must defend themselves.
    • Roman Britain split into separate kingdoms, but the Romano-Celts continued to fight the Saxon raiders.
  • Roman Britain: life and legacy
    • After the first century AD the Celts who lived in what is now England were to a certain extent Romanised; many towns appeared — some were created deliberately, others grew up by Roman forts as the garrisons provided markets for townspeople’s goods.
    • Roman towns were usually laid out in a grid pattern; streets were often covered in gravel; at first unprotected, then in the late 1st and 2nd centuries fortifications were built — at first ditches with earth ramparts and wooden palisades, later many towns had stone walls.
    • In the centre was a rectangular space called the Forum, lined by shops and by a public building called the basilica; markets were also held on the forum; another important building was the public baths — people went not just to get clean but also to socialise; baths consisted of a frigidarium (cold room), tepidarium (warm room) and caldarium (hot room), usually finishing with a dip in a cold pool.
    • Larger towns also had an amphitheatre where “sports” such as cock fighting were held and sometimes gladiators fought to the death; some towns also had theatres.
    • The Romans gambled with dice and played board games; children played with wooden or clay dolls and ball games; in the late 1st and 2nd centuries they practised cremation, but in the 3rd century began to bury the dead in cemeteries outside the walls; many towns also had public toilets.
    • Roman Britain was an agricultural society where most people made their living from farming (although there were many craftsmen); only a small minority of the population (probably around 10%) lived in towns.
    • Upper-class Celts adopted the Roman way of life: they built villas modelled on Roman buildings and enjoyed luxuries such as mosaics and even a form of central heating called a hypocaust; wealthy Romans also had wall paintings (murals) and panes of glass in their windows.
    • Poorer Romans had none of these things: their houses were simple and plain, the main form of heating was braziers, and oil lamps were used for light; some people had a piped water supply — water was brought into towns in aqueducts and went along lead pipes to individual houses.
    • Education: sons and daughters of well-to-do Romans went to primary school to learn to read and write and do simple arithmetic; only boys went to secondary school, where they learned geometry, history, literature and oratory (the art of public speaking).
    • Roman boys and girls wore a kind of necklace called a bulla — a charm inside a pouch worn around the neck; a boy wore his until he reached manhood, a girl discarded hers when she got married; for rich children the bulla might be made of gold.
    • Food: for the rich luxuries like wine and olive oil were imported; the Romans also introduced new foods into Britain — celery, cabbages, radishes, carrots, cucumbers, broad beans and walnuts; they cooked on charcoal stoves.
    • The Romans are famous for the network of roads they built across Britain, which remained centuries after they departed; rich people travelled by horse or on long journeys by covered wagon, and sometimes were carried in litters (seats between two long poles).
    • Religion: the Romans were usually tolerant, but they destroyed the Druids, who had great political and social influence and were probably seen as a threat; by the second century there were Christians in Roman Britain, and the Romans persecuted them; persecution ended in 312 when Constantine converted, and in 395 Christianity became the official religion of the Roman Empire.
    • However, although the Druid priesthood was abolished, the Celtic people continued to worship their traditional gods and goddesses; the Romans introduced their own gods, but they were often very similar to the Celtic ones.
  • Anglo-Saxon England: invasion, conversion and the Vikings
    • c. 450 invasions by Angles, Saxons and Jutes begin; 477 according to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle Saxons led by Aella land in Sussex near Pevensey.
    • c. 520 the Celts utterly defeat the Saxons at the battle of Mount Badon, somewhere in Somerset, halting their advance for decades.
    • 577 the Saxons defeat the Romano-Celts at Deorham; afterwards they capture Bath, Cirencester and Gloucester, cutting the Celts in Wales off from the Celts in south-west England.
    • 597 St Augustine arrives in Kent and begins to convert the Saxons; 601 Augustine becomes the first Archbishop of Canterbury; 604 the first Bishop of London is appointed; 664 the Synod (church meeting) at Whitby; 735 the Venerable Bede dies.
    • 757–796 Offa reigns in Mercia; 793 the first Viking raid — they sack the monastery at Lindisfarne; 865 the Danes invade England.
    • 871 the Saxons defeat the Danes at Ashdown and Alfred the Great becomes king of Wessex; 878 Alfred crushes the Danes at the battle of Edington.
    • 879 by the Treaty of Wedmore England is split between the Saxons and the Danes — Watling Street forms part of the border and London is left in Danish hands; 886 Alfred recaptures London; 899 Alfred dies aged 50.
    • 899–924 the reign of Edward the Elder: in 901 he calls himself “King of the Angles and Saxons”, in 913 he recaptures Essex from the Danes, and over time the Saxon kings take over all of the Danish territory (the Danelaw).
    • 924–939 the battle of Brunanburh, where the English defeat an army of Danes, Scots and Irish.
    • Reigns: Edmund 939–946, Edred 946–955, Edwy 955–959, Edgar 959–975, Edward the Martyr 975–978 (stabbed to death in Dorset), Ethelred 978–1016, Canute 1016–1035, Harold I 1035–1040, Hardicanute 1040–1042, Edward the Confessor 1042–1066.
  • Anglo-Saxon England: everyday life and society
    • Everyday life in Saxon times was hard and rough even for the rich; society was divided into three classes: at the top the thanes (the Saxon upper class, who enjoyed hunting and feasting and were expected to give their followers gifts like weapons); below them the churls (some reasonably well off, others very poor, but at least free); below them a class of slaves called thralls, whose lives were very hard.
    • Some churls owned their own land, but many “rented” land from a thane and “paid rent” by working on the thane’s land for part of the week and by giving him part of their crops.
    • The human population was very small — perhaps one million people in England at that time; almost all lived in tiny villages, many with fewer than 100 inhabitants; each village was mainly self-sufficient and people needed only a few things from outside like salt and iron.
    • By the 11th century things had changed somewhat: the great majority still lived in the countryside, but a significant minority (about 10%) lived in towns; many new towns had been created and trade was flourishing; England had grown into a stable, civilised state with an efficient system of local government, and in the monasteries learning and the arts flourished.
    • The Saxons gave us most English place names: -ham (village or estate), -tun/-ton (farm or estate), -hurst (wooded hill), -bury (from burh, fortress or fortified settlement); the Saxons called groups of Roman buildings a caester, which evolved into the endings -chester, -caster, -cester.
    • At first Saxon society was relatively free: there were some slaves, but the basis of society was the free peasant; however in time churls began to lose their freedom and became increasingly dependent on their lords.
    • Farming: up to 8 oxen pulled ploughs; fields were divided into 2 or sometimes 3 huge strips — one ploughed and sown with crops while the other was left fallow; the Saxons grew wheat, barley and rye, and also peas, cabbages, parsnips, carrots and celery; they ate fruit such as apples, blackberries, raspberries and sloes, and raised herds of goats, cattle and pigs and flocks of sheep.
    • However farming was very primitive: farmers could not grow enough food to keep many animals through the winter, so as winter approached most had to be slaughtered and the meat salted; the Saxons were subsistence farmers, growing enough to feed themselves and very little else, and at times there were terrible famines when poor people starved to death.
    • Craftsmen included blacksmiths, bronze smiths and potters (at first hand-made vessels, but in the 7th century the potter’s wheel was introduced); other craftsmen made combs from bone, antler or horn; there were many leather workers, and craftsmen also made elaborate jewellery for the rich.
    • Houses: the Saxons lived in wooden huts with thatched roofs; usually there was only one room shared by everybody; poor people shared their huts with animals divided from them by a screen — during winter the animals’ body heat helped keep the hut warm; thanes and their followers slept on beds but the poorest slept on the floor.
    • There were no panes of glass in windows, even in a thane’s hall, and no chimneys — smoke escaped through a hole in the roof; floors were of earth or sometimes dug out with wooden floorboards placed over them; there were no carpets; rich people used candles but they were too expensive for the poor, who used rush lights (rushes dipped in animal fat).
    • Saxon toilets were just pits dug in the ground surrounded by walls of wattle (strips of wood woven together); the seat was a piece of wood with a hole in it.
    • Women ground grain, baked bread and brewed beer; another drink was mead, made from fermented honey — honey was very important as there was no sugar for sweetening food, and bees were kept in every village; upper-class Saxons sometimes drank wine.
    • Women cooked in iron cauldrons over open fires or in pottery vessels, made butter and cheese; Saxons ate from wooden bowls, had no forks only knives and wooden spoons, and cups were made from cow horn.
    • Saxons were fond of meat and fish, but meat was a luxury only the rich could eat frequently; ordinary people usually ate a dreary diet of bread, cheese and eggs — not just chickens’ eggs but eggs from ducks, geese and wild birds.
    • Clothing: men wore a shirt and tunic, trouser-like garments called breeches (sometimes to the ankle, sometimes shorts), wool leggings held in place by leather garters, and cloaks held in place by brooches; women wore a long linen garment with a long tunic over it, and also mantles; both men and women used combs made of bone or antler.
  • Anglo-Saxon England: the tribes
    • Picts — a confederation of Celtic tribes living in what was later eastern and northern Scotland; Pictland (Pictavia) gradually merged with the Gaelic kingdom of Dál Riata to form the Kingdom of Alba (Scotland); recorded history begins in the Dark Ages; they were farmers living in small communities, cattle and horses were obvious signs of wealth and prestige, sheep and pigs were kept in large numbers; the early Picts are associated with piracy and raiding along the coasts of Roman Britain, and even in the Late Middle Ages the line between traders and pirates was unclear.
    • Scots — a nation and ethnic group native to Scotland; historically they emerged from an amalgamation of the Celtic Picts (east) and the Gaels (west), incorporating neighbouring Britons as well as Germanic peoples such as the Anglo-Saxons and the Norse; in the Early Middle Ages Scotland had several ethnic or cultural groups — Picts, Gaels, Britons, with Angles settling in the far south-east in smaller numbers; culturally these peoples are grouped according to language, and most of Scotland until the 13th century spoke Celtic languages.
    • Saxons — a confederation of Old Germanic tribes who participated in the Germanic settlement of Britain during and after the 5th century; the pre-Christian settlement originally covered an area a little more to the north-west, with parts of the southern Jutland Peninsula, Old Saxony and small sections of the eastern Low Countries; all mentions during the 4th and early 5th centuries referred to pirates and warlords in Gaul and Britain rather than to a specific tribe; Saxon raiders had been harassing the eastern and southern shores of Britannia for centuries, prompting the construction of the Saxon Shore forts, and many Saxons had been permitted to settle as farmers long before the end of Roman rule; according to tradition they first entered Britain en masse as part of a deal to protect the Britons from the Picts, Irish and others; the Saxons have no king but are governed by several ealdormen who during war cast lots for leadership but in peace are equal in power, and they held an annual council at Marklo where they confirmed laws, gave judgment on outstanding cases and determined by common counsel whether they would go to war or be in peace that year.
    • Angles — a modern English word for a Germanic-speaking people who took their name from the ancestral cultural region of Angeln, a district in Schleswig-Holstein, Germany; they were one of the main groups that settled in Britain in the post-Roman period, founding several of the kingdoms of Anglo-Saxon England, and their name is the root of the name “England”.
    • Jutes — believed to have originated from Jutland (Iutum in Latin) in modern Denmark, Southern Schleswig and part of the East Frisian coast; together with some Angles, Saxons and Frisians they sailed across the North Sea to raid and eventually invade Great Britain from the late 4th century onwards, displacing, absorbing or destroying the native Celtic peoples; according to Bede they finally settled in Kent (where they became known as the Cantuarii), Hampshire (in Wessex) and the Isle of Wight (Uictuarii).
  • Historic figures: Alfred the Great
    • As a young man Alfred had already shown promise, and many stories depict him as a bright child — stories that could have come about as part of a long propaganda struggle; the earliest tale describes his mother Osburh offering a beautifully decorated book to the first of her sons who could read it to her; even though Alfred was the youngest son, he was the first to read the book and so won the prize.
    • There are also reports of Alfred committing Saxon poems to memory and telling his biographer Asser that he resented not being taught to read and write properly until he was in his teens; although bright and studious he was also physically strong and enjoyed hunting, and socially he was portrayed as confident.
    • Alfred was only five when his father Aethelwulf sent him to the court of Pope Leo IV in Rome; he returned with the title of Consul, probably more a political title than a recognition of precocious genius; two years later he spent a year in Rome with his father, which at the very least widened the boy’s perspective and could have been good training for the life he was later to lead — he would need to be a soldier, diplomat and administrator.
    • In the 870s Alfred had to call on all his abilities: knowing that his victory at Ashdown was unlikely to slow the Vikings down for long, and working from his capital at Winchester, he set out to overhaul the defences of Wessex.
    • Up until this point men were called up in times of emergency and then returned to their jobs on the land — adequate for short-lived raids but not for a long campaign, and the Wessex army simply dissolved at harvest time.
    • In place of emergency call-up Alfred organised a system where half the men of fighting age stayed on the land while the other half did military service; in the event of a long campaign the original troops would be relieved by the men in reserve without disrupting farming activity; he also built a number of fortified locations called burhs.
    • Alfred’s policy was simply to resist, to keep on fighting, hoping the Great Army would then turn away to find easier prey.
    • In spite of his careful preparations, 878 saw Wessex on the verge of defeat: in January Danish leader Guthrum devised an audacious plan to attack in the middle of winter, something the Great Army had never done before; on Twelfth Night a Viking attack was launched against the royal Wessex town of Chippenham; Alfred was forced to flee and wage a guerrilla war from the swamps of Athelney; indignities of this time apparently include Alfred being reprimanded by a swine herd’s wife for burning her cakes.
    • By the spring of 878 news that Alfred was still alive was spreading, and within weeks he had managed to organise a resistance; he took command of an army that gathered at King Egbert’s Stone on the borders of Wiltshire and Somerset (the exact location is unclear, but local legend suggests Kingsettle Hill in Wiltshire, where in the 1760s Henry Hoare built a commemorative tower).
    • In the battle at Edington, twelve miles from Kingsettle Hill, the Viking chieftain Guthrum was defeated; perhaps even more significant than the military victory were Alfred’s diplomatic moves: he invited Guthrum into his tent, won him over and persuaded him to accept baptism; the resulting alliance gave Alfred and Anglo-Saxon England breathing space.
    • This alliance illustrates the depth of Alfred’s character: he was not simply a warrior king but an educated man who translated the Psalms, created law codes and set up schools for the ruling classes; he believed that exercise of power should go together with possession of knowledge; towards the end of his life he translated five works of Latin into English, and saw himself as beginning the building of something.
  • Historic figures: Edward the Confessor
    • Edward, later known as the Confessor in recognition of his pious ways, was crowned in Winchester on Easter Day 1043.
    • In his first year as king Edward had to secure his kingdom against the threat of invasion from Magnus of Norway, who was supported by Emma, Edward’s mother and second wife of Ethelred.
    • In 1043 Edward moved decisively: he sent soldiers to Winchester to seize Emma’s huge reserve of treasure, which effectively broke her power and she lived the rest of her life quietly; every year until 1047 Magnus continued to pose a threat, and every year Edward took personal command of the fleet at Sandwich ready to oppose invasion.
    • Just as dangerous was a threat to Edward’s authority from within: Earl Godwine of Wessex was powerful and ambitious, and in the early days of his reign Edward had no choice but to seek his support — always an uneasy relationship.
    • Edward tried to consolidate Godwine’s support by marrying his daughter Edith, and he placed Godwine’s sons Harold and Tosti in important positions of power, which was not too much of an imposition as both were talented soldiers and administrators.
    • Godwine’s eldest son Swein was a much more difficult proposition: after exile for abducting an abbess and murdering his cousin Beorn, Swein was to be the cause of much disagreement between Godwine and Edward; a difficult situation was made even more complicated when it became clear that Edith would not be able to have children — to secure the succession Edward needed a son, which meant divorce from Edith, but divorcing Edith was not straightforward when she was the daughter of Godwine.
    • Eventually a showdown had to come, in July 1051, when Edward’s brother-in-law Count Eustace of Boulogne visited England; on his way back to the continent Eustace and his party got into a fight with the townspeople of Dover.
    • As Dover was in the part of the country administered by Godwine, the earl was given the job of ravaging Dover in retribution for the riot — and Godwine refused.
    • According to the historian Ian Walker it is quite possible that Edward engineered the riot to bring a confrontation with Godwine: Eustace and his men put their armour on before entering Dover, as though they expected trouble; having brought about the riot, Godwine could then be asked to inflict the traditional punishment, with Godwine being expected to refuse — if this was the plan it worked perfectly.
    • Edward summoned a meeting of the royal council for 8 September to deal with Godwine’s insubordination; Godwine realised he was in real trouble and decided to call out his forces, and his sons Swein and Harold did the same; both sides, however, shied away from civil war, and support for Godwine started to dwindle.
    • Godwine was forced into exile, leaving England from his estate at Bosham in Sussex and heading for Flanders, while Harold made for Ireland; free of Godwine’s influence Edward quickly sent Edith to a nunnery at Wilton — an action fairly obviously motivated by Edward’s desire to find a new wife and secure the succession.
    • England was about to suffer its most famous invasion, but in the few years just before this shattering event the last part of Edward’s reign ironically saw the laying of important symbolic foundations of state at Westminster Abbey and Westminster Hall; Westminster Abbey was consecrated on 28 December 1065 but Edward was too ill to attend; he died just over a week later on 5 January 1066 and was buried at Westminster Abbey on the 6th.

Seminar 2 — Norman England · Domesday Book · Plantagenets · Magna Carta

Central concept: the Norman takeover and the legal-constitutional foundations of medieval England (1066–1485).

  • Norman England: timeline of the succession crisis
    • 1066 Harold becomes king, although William Duke of Normandy also claims the throne; Harald Hardrada of Norway claims it as well and invades England, but his army is crushed at the battle of Stamford Bridge in September; the Normans win the battle of Hastings in October; William the Conqueror is crowned king in December.
    • 1069–1070 the “Harrying of the North”: enraged by rebellion in the north of England, Norman soldiers burn houses and crops and kill livestock, leaving the area north of the Humber devastated.
    • 1086 the Domesday Book is compiled — it lists all the manors of England and their value; 1074 William creates the New Forest in Hampshire where he can go hunting.
    • 1087 William the Conqueror dies while at war and William Rufus becomes king; 1100 William Rufus is killed by an arrow while hunting in the New Forest (it is not clear whether it was an accident or murder) and Henry I becomes king.
    • 1120 the White Ship sinks in the English Channel and William, the heir to the English throne (Henry’s only legitimate son), drowns.
    • 1135 Henry I dies and there are two contenders: Henry wanted his daughter Matilda to rule, but many barons refuse to accept a woman ruler and support Henry’s nephew Stephen — the “19 long winters” of civil war begin.
    • 1138 the English defeat the Scots at the battle of the Standard; 1154 the civil wars end.
  • William the Conqueror: rise to power
    • William’s life was one of constant struggle in a turbulent world; although he rose from humble beginnings to rule Normandy and England, his authority was never safe; apart from his beloved wife Matilda he could trust virtually no one, not even his own sons.
    • No man had authority accepted by all others, so William did what many rulers had done before him: he appealed to a higher authority, working hard to give the impression that it was his God-given destiny to rule Normandy and England.
    • He was born at Falaise, Normandy, in 1027 or 1028, the illegitimate son of Robert, sixth Duke of what was then the independent province of Normandy; his mother was a girl from Falaise named Herleve, probably the daughter of a tanner; shortly after William’s birth Herleve was married off to Herlius, Vicomte of Conteville.
    • Little is known of William’s childhood; at the time there was no reason to take note of this little boy — in fact he was probably an embarrassment and might have been hidden away in Falaise.
    • It was not until 1034 that events suddenly began thrusting William towards a different life: to the fury of Normandy’s nobles, Duke Robert decided to go on a pilgrimage to Jerusalem; as the young duke had no heir, Robert persuaded his fellow nobles to accept William, the forgotten illegitimate child, as his heir — the nobles had little choice but to agree.
    • Robert died at Nicaea in modern Turkey in early July 1035, and unaccountably seven-year-old William the Bastard, as he was originally known, was Duke of Normandy.
    • A seven-year-old duke was not able to control Normandy, and initially Archbishop Robert of Rouen stepped in as a stabilising influence; but when he died in March 1037 the entire province descended into a state very near anarchy.
    • William lived through what must have been a terrifying time: tutors and advisors were being murdered all around him, and Osbern, a steward, was killed during a fight which actually took place in William’s bedroom.
    • Unrest in the autumn of 1046 led to a concerted effort to remove William; the story goes that an attempt to assassinate him was made while he was at Valognes, and a desperate night ride was required to escape to Falaise; William appealed to King Henry of France for protection, and a royal army marched into Normandy in early 1047 to rescue him from his enemies.
    • Perhaps the king preferred a helpless boy as duke rather than a stronger character who might cause problems; in spite of what later chroniclers might claim about the boy’s preternatural qualities, William might have survived not through his strength but because his weakness and youth meant King Henry did not consider him a threat.
    • The French invasion culminated in the Battle of Val-es-Dunes on the river Orne; William is described by chroniclers fighting bravely, which we have no reason to doubt, but this battle was King Henry’s victory; chroniclers writing later, when William was at the height of his power, claimed that the Normans saw William as their leader from this point.
  • William the Conqueror: consolidation and the English claim
    • Following Val-es-Dunes William had to endure years of virtually ceaseless warfare; between 1047 and 1049 he probably lost control of his own capital of Rouen to Guy of Burgundy, and in 1052 the French king decided to ally himself with one of William’s most dangerous enemies, Geoffrey Count of Anjou.
    • Perhaps William was no longer the helpless young boy he used to be: now a married man (he married Matilda of Flanders in 1053), he seemed to be viewed as a potential threat, and the young Duke’s former supporter became his enemy, which led to an acute crisis in Normandy.
    • Later chroniclers claimed William’s break with Henry as a proud moment of Norman independence; in fact William tried his best to prevent the split but was swept along by a general insistence that Normandy had to stand up to France.
    • In 1054 King Henry and his allies attempted to invade Normandy; by now William accepted that he had to fight and did so resolutely, defeating the French army at the Battle of Mortemer.
    • It was now that William showed his qualities as a leader: he was an effective organiser and combined ruthlessness with leniency in a psychologically powerful good cop/bad cop routine, which often broke enemies without a fight.
    • More attacks followed, but William managed to survive them, and with King Henry’s death in 1060 his position became a little more secure; a monastic revival also helped unify the once chaotic province, with military and religious authority going hand in hand — a characteristic feature of William’s reign; in a situation where no one would accept one man’s authority, an attempt was made to appeal to a higher authority beyond humanity.
    • William’s claim to the English throne is complicated and still controversial; it is difficult to tell the reality of what happened from the propaganda that justified the Norman invasion after it happened.
    • Did William visit Edward the Confessor in 1051 and receive an offer of the English throne? This is very unlikely, since William was rather busy at the time fighting for survival in Normandy; nevertheless he began to think of himself as Edward’s natural successor, so some kind of offer could have been made.
    • What exactly did the embittered, exiled Archbishop of Canterbury Robert of Jumieges say when he visited William in 1052? Perhaps in seeking support Robert mentioned his job of crowning English kings and how he would like to crown William — William could easily have taken this as an endorsement.
    • When England’s most powerful noble Harold Godwineson was washed up at Ponthieu near Normandy in 1064, what exactly was he doing? He could have been on a mission from Edward to offer the English throne to William, he could have been looking for a wife, or seeking the release of family members held by William; it is now very hard to tell, because later writers might well have written about the past in a way that was convenient for the future — propaganda is the portrayal of past events to make them appear inevitable.
    • William was an incredible character, very resilient and determined; part of his accomplishment was to give the illusion of authority and order where none existed — perhaps this is the work of any government; nothing destined little William the Bastard, son of Herleve, daughter of a Falaise tanner, to be the Conqueror — luck played a large part in events.
  • Domesday Book: nature and structure
    • Domesday is the most comprehensive survey of land tenure in England ever produced by a medieval king; it is really two independent works: Little Domesday covers Norfolk, Suffolk and Essex, while Great Domesday covers the rest of England, except for lands in the north that would later become Westmorland, Cumberland, Northumberland and County Durham.
    • There are also no surveys of London, Winchester and some other towns — the omission of these two major cities is probably due to their size and complexity.
    • Despite its name, Little Domesday was actually larger as it is far more detailed, down to numbers of livestock; it has been suggested that Little Domesday represents a first attempt, and that it was found impossible, or at least inconvenient, to complete the work on the same scale for Great Domesday.
    • In each county the list opened with the holdings of the king himself (which had possibly formed the subject of a separate inquiry); then came those of the churchmen and religious houses in order of status (for example, the Archbishop of Canterbury is always listed before other bishops); next were entered those of the lay tenants-in-chief again in approximate order of status (aristocrats); and then the king’s serjeants and English thegns who retained land.
    • Domesday names a total of 13,418 places.
  • Domesday Book: purposes, limitations and significance
    • The importance of Domesday for understanding the period is difficult to overstate; as H.C. Darby noted, anyone who uses it “can have nothing but admiration for what is the oldest ‘public record’ in England and probably the most remarkable statistical document in the history of Europe”; the continent has no document to compare with this detailed description covering so great a stretch of territory.
    • On the other hand, Darby points out that when this great wealth of data is examined more closely, “perplexities and difficulties arise”: the clerks who compiled the document were but human and were frequently forgetful or confused; the use of Roman numerals also led to countless mistakes; but more importantly there are numerous obvious omissions and ambiguities in the presentation of the material.
    • William’s reasons for compiling the Domesday Book will never be known for certain; however it was probably the result of a geld (land tax) inquest to raise funds for the realm’s defence — in 1085 the kingdom had come under threat from King Canute of Denmark and King Olaf of Norway, and unrest in France, Normandy and Scotland contributed to an uneasy winter; geld was collected in the year of Domesday.
    • Others suggest William wanted an account of the lands held by his tenants-in-chief so he could exercise his rights as feudal overlord; there is also the school of thought that he wanted to assess the burden of mercenaries upon his vassals and redistribute the burden fairly.
    • A newer theory is that several years lapsed between the inquest and the making of Domesday, and that it was written after the death of William and commissioned by his son William II as a result of a revolt in 1088; some have also suggested William simply wished to learn more about the country he had conquered 20 years earlier and to bring order from the chaos of the Conquest — “To bring the conquered people under the rule of written law”, wrote one chronicler.
    • Domesday tells us who owned the land before 1066, who owned it in 1086, how it changed hands, what the land was worth and what manors it was associated with, and how many peasants (bordars and villani) tended the land.
    • This was achieved through judicious use of Anglo-Saxon local government: Anglo-Norman England was “governed” by local officers — the sheriff and the reeve, based within the shire; people cooperated with them and took part in the shire courts, because the whole community’s existence depended on the efficient running of the shire.
    • William formed an alliance with the freemen of the shire to run the country based upon their autonomous local governance, through the agency of the sheriff, who formed the natural link between shire and king; this created a dynamic tension between the lords, the shire and the king, which sometimes erupted into resentment from the earls, as in the revolt of Edwin and Morcar or the Revolt of the Earls in 1075.
    • It is also possible to see that the Domesday commissioners were themselves basically confused about what a manor actually was — their concept of a manor changes throughout the book.
    • What Domesday shows is that the Norman barony had taken over the role of the English thegns: the two are almost interchangeable — both held land in return for service (mainly military), both had sub-tenants, and both owed their ultimate position, and therefore their allegiance, to the king; in many cases all that had happened was that the barons had been inserted as an extra social layer between the English king and the English thegnly class.
  • Domesday Book: land transfer and the peasantry
    • Antecession — all the lands of one person were given by the King to a Norman.
    • Castleries — these and the rapes of the south were a cluster of lands granted around a central castle, which the holder was expected to build and maintain.
    • Official forfeiture — a favourite of sheriffs: any land under dispute could be expropriated into the “official” patrimony of the local officer.
    • Naked aggression — the lord fancies a piece of land and simply takes it, in one of two ways: first, simply paying the tax on it (mainly to do with rents in kind, e.g. by telling the reeve to pay the rents to the lord rather than to the original landowner — there was supposed to be a three-day grace period between paying rent and the passing-over of title); second, sending in the bully-boys and kicking the current owner off the land, which was probably very rare, as lords wanted land because they wanted the rents from it and therefore needed someone on it to pay those rents.
    • Marriage — Norman knights who gained title to lands by marrying the widow or daughter who owned them, often with the active blessing of the King.
    • The peasantry — the people who actually tilled the land — were basically divided into villani and bordars.
    • The villani (villeins) were the aristocracy of the peasants: they could own a large chunk of land in the local area, up to a hide or more, in return for labour-service to the lord; it is important not to confuse them with the villeins they eventually turned into during the Middle Ages, because at the time of Domesday the villani were essentially free and paying Geld (the basic definition of freedom), which the villeins were not doing by the end of the 12th century.
    • Bordars (cottars) are a bit more problematic — nobody is quite sure what their status was: they were either people “assarting” (farming) peripheral land, or members of the lord’s demesne who farmed an area in return for wages, which included the right to food from the lord’s table.
    • So Domesday is a huge administrative document which throws light on some of the more basic, nitty-gritty aspects of the takeover of England by its Norman conquerors; from it we can also glean a whole series of personal stories and experiences of life under the new régime.
    • Extract terms: hundred (part of a county, a piece of land 100 hides in size); hide (land large enough for one family, between 40 and 120 acres); vill (small village); demesne (land only the earl or lord used, on which people had to work for him); virgate (a quarter of a hide); villeins (higher class of unfree peasant who owned land but also had to work on the earl’s land); cottar (unfree peasant with little or no land, probably living in a cottage outside the main manor); TRE (Latin abbreviation meaning “In the name of King Edward”).

  • Magna Carta: real significance and content
    • Magna Carta is one of the most celebrated documents in English history, but later interpretations have tended to obscure its real significance in 1215; this iconic document was not intended to be a lasting declaration of legal principle — it was a practical solution to a political crisis which primarily served the interests of the highest ranks of feudal society by reasserting the power of custom to limit despotic behaviour by the king.
    • Only three of the 63 original clauses are still law: one defends the freedom and rights of the English church, another confirms the liberties and customs of London and other towns, but the third is the most famous:
    • “No free man shall be seized or imprisoned, or stripped of his rights or possessions, or outlawed or exiled, nor will we proceed with force against him except by the lawful judgement of his equals or by the law of the land. To no one will we sell, to no one deny or delay right or justice.”
    • This statement of principle, buried deep in Magna Carta, was given no particular prominence in 1215, but its intrinsic adaptability has allowed succeeding generations to reinterpret it for their own purposes, which has ensured its longevity.
    • In the fourteenth century Parliament saw it as guaranteeing trial by jury; Sir Edward Coke interpreted it as a declaration of individual liberty in his conflict with the early Stuart kings; and it has resonant echoes in the American Bill of Rights and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.
    • But the real legacy of Magna Carta as a whole is that it limited the king’s authority by establishing the crucial principle that the law was a power in its own right to which the king was subject.
    • Physical document: written on parchment, not paper — parchment was the normal writing material in England until the end of the Middle Ages, made from sheepskin soaked in a bath of lime, stretched on a frame to dry under tension, then scraped with a curved knife to produce a smooth writing surface for scribes who wrote with a quill pen.
    • Like other medieval royal charters, Magna Carta was authenticated with the Great Seal, not by the signature of the King — in fact there is no evidence that King John could write at all; there is no evidence that a single Magna Carta was written up and sealed when John met the barons at Runnymede in 1215; instead, once the terms were finalised, the barons renewed their oaths of allegiance to the king.
    • “Magna Carta” means “The Great Charter”; in 1215 it was known as the Charter of Liberties, but when it was confirmed in 1217 the clauses dealing with the law of the royal forest were taken out and put into a separate document known as “The Charter of the Forest”; after that people began to refer to the Charter of Liberties as Magna Carta to distinguish it from the shorter Charter of the Forest.
    • Despite what many people expect, Magna Carta includes very few statements of legal principle; in fact the majority of the 63 clauses deal with the detail of feudal rights and customs and the administration of justice — it was King John’s excessive and arbitrary exploitation of his feudal rights, and his abuse of the justice system, which more than anything else had fuelled the barons’ rebellion in the first place.
    • Magna Carta was the first grant by an English king to set detailed limits on royal authority; through its statement of liberties it sought to prevent the king from exploiting his power in arbitrary ways, and it made clear that the king was subject to the law, not above it.
  • De Montfort’s Parliament and William Caxton
    • De Montfort’s Parliament was an English parliament of 1265 instigated by Simon de Montfort, a baronial rebel leader; although this gathering did not have the approval of King Henry III and the members convened without royal approval, most scholars believe this was the first gathering in England that can be called a parliament in the dictionary sense of the word.
    • De Montfort’s army had met and defeated the royal forces at the Battle of Lewes on 14 May 1264; the rebels captured the king’s son and heir Prince Edward, and the subsequent treaty led to a parliament being called in 1265 to agree to a constitution formulated by De Montfort.
    • De Montfort sent out representatives to each county and to a select list of boroughs, asking each to send two representatives; he insisted the representatives be elected; this was also the first parliament at which both knights (representing shires or counties) and burgesses (representing boroughs) were present, thereby substantially broadening representation to include new groups of society, and the first time that commoners attending Parliament were required to be elected.
    • De Montfort’s Parliament was summoned on 14 December 1264, first met on 20 January 1265 and was dissolved on 15 February 1265; Henry III rejected the new Parliament and resumed his war against De Montfort, who was killed later that year; a field near Kenilworth Castle in Warwickshire, known as Parliament Piece, is where the first parliament was held — a protected site which cannot be built upon.
    • William Caxton (c. 1422–1492) was the first English printer and a translator and importer of books into England; he was born around 1422 in Kent and went to London at the age of 16 to become an apprentice to a merchant, later moving to Bruges, the centre of the wool trade, where he became a successful and important member of the merchant community.
    • From 1462 to 1470 he served as governor of the “English Nation of Merchant Adventurers”, which allowed him to represent his fellow merchants as well as act as a diplomat for the king.
    • Caxton affiliated himself with the household of Margaret, Duchess of Burgundy, sister of the English king Edward IV; she became one of his most important patrons and encouraged him with his translation of “The Recuyell of the Histories of Troye” from French to English.
    • In the early 1470s Caxton spent time in Cologne learning the art of printing; he returned to Bruges in 1472 where he and Colard Mansion, a Flemish calligrapher, set up a press; Caxton’s own translation of “The Recuyell of the Histories of Troye” was the first book printed in the English language.
    • In 1476 Caxton returned to London and established a press at Westminster — the first printing press in England; among the books he printed were Chaucer’s “Canterbury Tales”, Gower’s “Confessio Amantis” and Malory’s “Le Morte d’Arthur”.
    • He printed more than 100 books in his lifetime, known for their craftsmanship and careful editing; he was also the translator of many of the books he published, using his knowledge of French, Latin and Dutch; he died in 1492.

  • Tudor society, population and the vagrancy laws
    • Tudor England was an agricultural society: most of the population lived in small villages and made their living from farming; during the 16th century trade and industry grew rapidly and England became more and more commercial; mining of coal, tin and lead flourished, so did the iron industry, and England became richer and richer.
    • However there were winners and losers: upper-class and middle-class Tudors saw a big rise in their standard of living and the homes of the well off became more and more comfortable, but the lowest section of society — the wage labourers — became worse off.
    • Population: in the 15th century it may have been around 2.5 million; by 1525 it had risen to around 3 million and by 1600 it was about 4 million.
    • During the 16th century there was inflation, especially in mid-century, and prices rose steeply; wages rose too but less than prices so real earnings fell, reaching their lowest point in 1597; the years 1594–97 were ones of famine, and in Cumbria — the poorest and most isolated part of England — people starved to death.
    • Tudor society was divided into four broad groups: at the top the nobility, who owned huge amounts of land; below them the gentry and rich merchants — gentlemen owned large amounts of land, were usually educated, had a family coat of arms and never did manual work that was beneath their dignity; below the gentry were yeomen and craftsmen — yeomen owned their own land, could be as wealthy as gentlemen but worked alongside their men, and yeomen and craftsmen were often able to read and write; below them were tenant farmers who leased land from the rich, and wage labourers, who were often illiterate and very poor.
    • In the 16th century about 50% of the population lived at subsistence level — just enough food, clothes and shelter to survive; for them life was very hard; however it was possible to move from one class to another: with hard work and luck a husbandman could become a yeoman, a yeoman could buy a coat of arms and become a gentleman, and it was possible for an ambitious young man to rise in the world.
    • With the rise in population jobs were not always easy to find: there were thousands of people without jobs wandering around looking for work, as well as disabled beggars.
    • 1530 law: the old and disabled poor were to be given licences to beg, but anyone roaming without a job was tied to a cart in the nearest market town and whipped till bloody, then forced to return to the parish where he had been born or where he had lived for the last 3 years.
    • 1536 law: vagabonds were whipped the first time; for a second offence part of their right ear was cut off (so they could be easily identified); for a third offence they were hanged; however officers of the law were reluctant to carry out such draconian measures.
    • 1547 law chided them for “foolish pity and mercy”: anyone who roamed or loitered for 3 days without a job must offer to work for any employer for any wages he was willing to pay; if nobody would employ him he must offer to work just for food and drink; if he did not do this anyone could take him to the Justice of the Peace, and the vagabond was then made that person’s slave for 2 years; if he ran away he was branded and made a slave for life; this terrible law was abolished in 1550 and flogging was once again the punishment; every parish was commanded to build a workhouse for the old and disabled poor, who were housed there and made to do any work they were capable of.
    • 1572 law (more severe again): for a first offence a vagabond would be whipped and burned in the right ear with a red-hot iron (unless some kindly employer was willing to give him a job); for a second offence he would be hanged (again unless an employer gave him a job); for a third offence he would be hanged regardless.
    • 1576 law: parishes were ordered to supply the old and disabled with materials like flax, hemp, wool and iron so they could work at home; any old or disabled person who refused to work was sent to a House of Correction where conditions were very harsh; in 1597 the death penalty for vagrancy was abolished.
  • Tudor housing, furniture and domestic interiors
    • In the Middle Ages rich people’s houses were designed for defence rather than comfort; in the 16th century life was safer so houses no longer had to be easy to defend; rich Tudors built grand houses — Cardinal Wolsey built Hampton Court Palace, and later the Countess of Shrewsbury built Hardwick Hall in Derbyshire.
    • People below the rich but above the poor built sturdy “half-timbered” houses made with a timber frame filled in with wattle and daub (wickerwork and plaster); in the late 16th century some people built or rebuilt their houses with a wooden frame filled in with bricks; roofs were usually thatched, though some well-off people had tiles (in London all houses had tiles because of the fear of fire).
    • Furniture was more plentiful than in the Middle Ages but still basic; in a wealthy home it was usually made of oak and was heavy and massive; Tudor furniture was expected to last for generations; comfortable beds became more and more common, and in a middle-class home a mattress was often stuffed with flock (a kind of rough wool); chairs were more common than in the Middle Ages but still expensive — even in an upper-class home children and servants sat on stools, and the poor had to make do with stools and benches.
    • In the 15th century only a small minority could afford glass windows; in Tudor times they became much more common but were still expensive — if you moved house you took your glass windows with you; Tudor windows were made of small pieces of glass held together by strips of lead and were called lattice windows; the poor still had to make do with strips of linen soaked in linseed oil.
    • Chimneys were also a luxury, although they became more common; poor people simply had a hole in the roof; as more and more people could afford chimneys this had an important effect on homes: in the Middle Ages a well-to-do person’s house was dominated by the great hall, which extended all the way up to the roof where a hole let out smoke; it was not possible to build upstairs rooms over the great hall or the smoke would not be able to escape, but building chimneys meant that many people could now install another storey over the great hall, so well-off people’s houses became divided into more rooms.
    • In wealthy Tudor houses the walls of rooms were lined with oak panelling to keep out drafts; people slept in four-poster beds hung with curtains to reduce drafts; some people had wallpaper but it was very expensive; other wealthy people hung tapestries or painted cloths on their walls.
    • Carpets were a luxury only the richest could afford and were usually too expensive to put on the floor — instead they were often hung on the wall or over tables; people covered their floors with rushes or reeds (or mats of woven rushes or reeds), which they strewed with sweet-smelling herbs.
    • In the 16th century prosperous people lit their homes with beeswax candles, but they were expensive; others used candles made from tallow (animal fat), which gave off an unpleasant smell; rich Tudors were also fond of gardens — many had mazes, fountains and topiary (hedges cut into shapes), while less well-off people used their gardens to grow vegetables and herbs.
    • None of the improvements applied to the poor: they continued to live in simple huts with one or two rooms (occasionally three); smoke escaped through a hole in the thatched roof; floors were of hard earth and furniture was very basic — benches, stools, a table and wooden chests; they slept on mattresses stuffed with straw or thistledown, laid on ropes strung across a wooden frame.
    • In 1596 Sir John Harrington invented a flushing lavatory with a cistern, but the idea failed to catch on; people continued to use chamber pots or cess pits, which were cleaned by men called gong farmers (in the 16th century a toilet was called a jakes).
  • Tudor food and towns
    • Rich Tudors ate vast amounts of meat but rarely ate vegetables — they were not necessarily well fed because they ate too few vegetables; poor people ate plenty of vegetables because they had no choice, since vegetables were cheap but meat was a luxury.
    • On certain days by law people had to eat fish instead of meat: at first this was for religious reasons, but later in the 16th century it was to support the fishing industry; if you lived near the sea or a river you could eat fresh fish like herrings or mackerel, otherwise you might have to rely on dried or salted fish.
    • Poor Tudors lived on a dreary diet: in the morning bread and cheese and onions; only one cooked meal a day; they mixed grain with water and added vegetables and (if they could afford it) strips of meat.
    • All classes ate bread but it varied in quality: rich people’s bread was made from fine white flour, poor people ate coarse bread of barley or rye.
    • In the 16th century many people thought fresh fruit was bad for you; they did eat fruit but usually after it was cooked and made into a tart or pie; the Tudors were also fond of sweet foods, but sugar was very expensive so most people used honey to sweeten their food.
    • New foods were introduced from the Americas: turkeys about 1525, potatoes in the 1580s (at first few English people ate them), tomatoes from Mexico and apricots from Portugal.
    • Nobody drank water in Tudor times because it was too dirty; young children drank milk, everyone else drank ale or, if they were rich, wine; from the mid-16th century beer became common; the Tudors also drank cider and perry.
    • Rich people liked to show off their gold and silver plate; the middle classes had dishes and bowls made of pewter; the poor made do with wooden plates and bowls; there were no forks in Tudor times — people ate with knives and their fingers or with spoons; rich Tudors had silver or pewter spoons, the poor used wooden ones.
    • People made much of their own food: a farmer’s wife cured bacon and salted meat to preserve it, baked bread, brewed beer, made pickles and conserves and preserved vegetables; many prosperous farms kept bees for honey.
    • Towns: only a small part of the population of Tudor England lived in towns, but they played a vital role in the economy — peasants brought their surplus produce to weekly markets, and all kinds of manufactured goods like shoes and pottery were on sale.
    • Tudor London probably had a population of between 60,000 and 70,000; by 1600 its population was over 250,000; Bristol probably had about 14,000 in 1500 and about 20,000 by 1600; Norwich had about 10,000 in 1500 and still less than 20,000 in 1600; the largest town in the north, York, had about 10,000 in 1500 and only about 12,000 by 1600; the next largest was probably Exeter with about 9,000 in 1600; most towns were much smaller with populations of between 2,500 and 4,000 — in the 16th century anything with more than 1,000 inhabitants was considered a town.
    • In most Tudor towns tradesmen of one kind tended to live and work in the same street — e.g. in many towns butchers and slaughterhouses gathered together in a street called the Shambles.
    • Tudor towns were dirty, smelly and crowded: there were no sewers and no drains, and rubbish such as rotting vegetables, offal and dirty water was thrown in the streets; in some towns every man was supposed to clean the street in front of his house once a week but it is unlikely many people bothered; rats and other vermin were common.
    • In Tudor London there were a few public toilets by streams, but in most towns there were none and people went wherever they could find a convenient spot; people usually obtained water from wells or from water carriers who carried water in containers on their shoulders, and some towns had conduits which brought in water from the countryside for public use.
    • In some towns the principal streets were paved but most town streets were not; streets were also very narrow, with upper storeys of buildings jutting out over lower storeys — these were called jetties.
    • At night the streets were dark and dangerous; quite apart from the danger of being robbed it was easy to have an accident in dark, unpaved streets; in London you could hire a linkboy with a lamp to light your way, but many people avoided going out after dark.
    • Given the dirty condition of Tudor towns it is not surprising that outbreaks of plague were common: when plague struck it might kill 10%, 15% or even more of the population of a town; however towns always recovered, as there were always plenty of poor people in the countryside willing to come to towns in search of work.
  • Tudor games and clothes
    • Although the days of armoured knights were over, rich Tudors still enjoyed tournaments: contestants dressed in armour and rode horses, fighting with wooden lances and swords.
    • Rich Tudors also enjoyed hunting deer with bows and arrows (after it was killed the deer was eaten), and hawking — falcons were trained to kill other birds; however in Tudor times rich people did not hunt foxes.
    • The Tudors liked wrestling and “casting the bar”, which was like shot-putting but with an iron bar; they also played billiards (but not snooker, which is a 19th-century game).
    • Rich Tudors played board games like chess and backgammon (a backgammon set was found on the wreck of the Mary Rose and is the same as a modern one); they also played tennis with a leather ball stuffed with hair, bowls and skittles, and playing cards were popular.
    • All classes gambled: poor people gambled with dice and played games like shuffleboard (shove ha’penny) and nine men’s morris; the Tudors also played draughts and fox and geese.
    • Music and dancing were very popular; the printing press made books much cheaper, so reading was a popular pastime for rich Tudors.
    • Ordinary people played a rough version of football: there were no rules and the “pitch” was often a large area including woods and even streams; it was a very rough game and injuries like broken limbs were common.
    • Cruel “sports” like cockfighting were popular, as was bear baiting — a bear was chained to a post and dogs were trained to attack it; another popular entertainment was watching public executions, with large crowds turning out to watch criminals hanged.
    • Clothes: for rich Tudors fashion was important, while for the poor clothes had to be hardwearing and practical; all classes wore wool, but it varied in quality — the rich wore fine quality wool, the poor coarse wool.
    • Linen was used to make shirts and underwear, but only the rich could afford cotton and silk; rich Tudors embroidered their clothes with silk, gold or silver thread, and rich Tudor women wore silk stockings.
    • In the 16th century men wore short trouser-like garments called breeches and tight-fitting jackets called doublets; another jacket called a jerkin was worn over the doublet, and over the jerkin rich men wore a gown, or later in the 16th century a cloak or cape; instead of a doublet many workingmen wore a loose tunic, which was easier to work in, and some wore a leather jerkin called a buff-jerkin; men also wore stockings or woollen socks called hose.
    • Women wore a kind of petticoat called a smock or shift or chemise made of linen or wool, and a wool dress over it; a woman’s dress was made of two parts — a bodice or corset-like garment and a skirt; sleeves were held on with laces and could be detached; workingwomen wore a linen apron.
    • In the late 16th century many women wore a frame made of whale bone or wood under their dress called a farthingale; if they could not afford one they wore a padded roll around their waist called a bum roll.
    • In the 16th century women did not wear knickers, although men sometimes wore linen shorts.
    • In Tudor times everyone wore hats; poor women often wore a linen cap called a coif, and after 1572 by law all men except nobles had to wear a woollen cap on Sundays — a law passed to give the wool cap makers plenty of work.
    • In the 16th century buttons were usually for decoration — clothes were held together with laces or pins; furs included cat, rabbit, beaver, bear, badger and polecat.
    • Some women wore wigs — both Elizabeth and Mary Queen of Scots wore them, and when Mary was beheaded her wig came off.
    • From the 14th century to the mid-17th century sumptuary laws laid down what each class could and could not wear; in the 16th century complicated laws said that only persons of a certain rank could wear certain expensive materials such as velvet and silk; these laws made no difference to poor people, who could not afford “sumptuous” materials anyway; they were supposed to keep the better-off classes separate, so that you could tell which class somebody belonged to by his or her clothes, but the sumptuary laws proved unenforceable and many people simply ignored them.
  • Documents: Act of Supremacy (1534) and Act of Six Articles (1539)
    • The name “Act of Supremacy” is given to two separate acts of the English Parliament, one passed in 1534 and the other in 1559; both acts had the same purpose — to firmly establish the English monarch as the official head of the Church of England, supplanting the power of the Catholic Pope in Rome.
    • The 1534 Act recognized Henry VIII as the “Supreme Head of the Church of England” and required an oath of loyalty from English subjects that recognized his marriage to Anne Boleyn; it is still the legal authority of the Sovereign of the United Kingdom.
    • Henry abandoned Rome completely and appointed himself and his successors as the supreme rulers of the English church; he had been declared “Defender of the Faith” in 1521 by Pope Leo X for his pamphlet accusing Martin Luther of heresy, and Parliament later conferred this title upon Henry in 1544.
    • There were a number of reasons for this Act, primarily the need for Henry to obtain an annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon, because Catherine had not produced a son and heir; at the same time Henry had his eye on the wealth of the church, particularly the property of the monasteries — his lifestyle and his desire for military glory had left him in a precarious financial position.
    • Pope Clement VII still refused to grant the annulment, due to Catherine’s familial connections with the Holy Roman Emperor at the time (and fear caused by Charles V’s sacking of Rome in 1527); the Treasons Act was later passed: it provided that to disavow the Act of Supremacy and to deprive the King of his “dignity, title, or name” was to be considered treason — the Act effectively made it treasonable to support the authority of the Pope over the Church of England.
    • Henry VIII’s Act of Supremacy was repealed in 1554 in the reign of his Catholic daughter Mary I and reinstated by Mary’s Protestant sister Elizabeth I; Elizabeth declared herself Supreme Governor of the Church of England and instituted an Oath of Supremacy, requiring anyone taking public or church office to swear allegiance to the monarch as head of the Church and state; anyone refusing could be charged with treason.
    • There were three levels of penalties for refusal to take the Oath: a first refusal resulted in loss of all movable goods; a second offence could mean life in prison and a loss of all real estate possessions; a third offence would result in a charge of High Treason and death; a few years later the Oath was extended to include MPs and anyone taking a university degree.
    • Thus it was through the Second Act of Supremacy (1559) that Elizabeth I officially established the Anglican Church in England.
    • Act of Six Articles (1539): in 1538 three German theologians were sent to London and held conferences with the Anglican bishops and clergy in the archbishop’s palace at Lambeth for several months, presenting a number of Lutheran Articles as a basis of agreement.
    • Bishops Tunstall, Stokesley and others were not won over by these Protestant arguments and did everything they could to avoid agreement; they were willing to separate from Rome, but their plan was to unite with the Greek Church and not with the evangelical Protestants on the continent; the bishops also refused to eliminate what the Germans called the “Abuses” (e.g. private Masses, celibacy of the clergy, worship of angels) allowed by the Anglican Church; the king, unwilling to break with Catholic practices, dissolved the conference.
    • Henry had felt uneasy about the appearance of the Lutheran doctors and their theology within his kingdom; on 28 April 1539 Parliament met for the first time in three years; on 5 May the House of Lords created a committee with the customary religious balance to examine and determine doctrine; on 16 May the Duke of Norfolk had the House of Lords consider six controversial issues: transubstantiation, the reasonableness of withholding of the cup from the laity during communion, clerical celibacy, observance of vows of chastity, permission for private masses, and the importance of auricular confession.
    • These formed the basis of the Act of Six Articles; denial of transubstantiation was to be punished by burning without the opportunity to recant, while denial of any of the other articles was punishable by hanging or life imprisonment; the Act was passed by Parliament in June 1539 and was referred to as “the bloody whip with six strings” by many Protestants.
    • In 1539 the Act returned the Church to unambiguous Catholic orthodoxy apart from papal supremacy; this was an embarrassment to the Archbishop of Canterbury, Thomas Cranmer, whose marriage was an open secret at the time, and Cranmer moved his wife and children out of England to safety.
    • More significantly, under this act heresy again became a felony — a clear signal that Henry VIII would not tolerate those with radical religious views; Henry tried to establish a consensus between Protestants and conservatives, with Protestants punished for violating the Six Articles while papists were punished for denying the royal supremacy; until Henry’s death in 1547 the Act of Six Articles remained the basis of the Church’s faith, and after Henry’s death the articles were repealed by his son Edward VI.
  • Historic figure: Mary, Queen of Scots
    • Mary, Queen of Scots was one of the most fascinating and controversial monarchs of 16th-century Europe; at one time she claimed the crowns of four nations — Scotland, France, England and Ireland; her physical beauty and kind heart were acknowledged even by her enemies, yet she lacked the political skills to rule successfully in Scotland.
    • Born in Linlithgow Palace in 1542, Mary was the third child (two young brothers had previously died) and the only daughter of James V of Scotland and his second wife Mary of Guise, a French noblewoman; her father died six days after her birth and with his death Mary became the infant queen.
    • On 23 July 1543 she and her mother were moved to the safety of Stirling Castle, where she was crowned Queen on 9 September; the young queen was then entrusted to the care and protection of Lords Erskine and Livingstone.
    • In an effort to create an alliance with Scotland, King Henry VIII proposed a marriage between his young son Edward and the infant Mary; when his efforts failed he launched a war on Scotland known as “The Rough Wooing” (1544–1551).
    • After Henry VIII died in 1547, young Edward’s uncle the Duke of Somerset continued the attempt to forcibly create an alliance, which saw several invasions into Scotland; shortly after the last invasion, the battle of Pinkie Cleugh, where the Scots suffered a bitter defeat, Mary’s mother sent her for security to the priory of Inchmahome on an island in the Lake of Menteith; she was eventually transferred to Dumbarton Castle, the stronghold most accessible to France.
    • Ten years later she married Francis, son of Henry II of France and Catherine de Medici; unfortunately this marriage did not last long as Francis died after only 2 years, and Mary was inconsolable.
    • In 1565 Mary married her cousin Henry Stuart, Lord Darnley, an English nobleman; the bridegroom was proclaimed Henry, King of Scots, and their only child was to become James I of England.
    • Mary soon became disenchanted with Henry: he had become overbearing, arrogant and carried away by his new title; he made enemies of some of the powerful nobles and, because of that enmity, there was a plot to kill him; some thought that Mary had knowledge of the plot; Henry, along with his servant, was found strangled to death after the gunpowder blast intended to take his life failed.
    • The rift between Mary and her husband became public knowledge; she turned to a Scottish nobleman, the Earl of Bothwell, for support, and he and other Scottish noblemen proposed to do whatever they could to help the queen in her dilemma; this decision led to a failed explosion plot and to the strangulation death of Darnley.
    • A few months later Mary and the Earl married, which angered the populace, who suspected Bothwell’s participation in the murder of their King; Mary’s subjects were outraged and turned against her.
    • She fled to England to ask for help from her cousin Queen Elizabeth I, but instead she was imprisoned; after 19 years of trying to obtain her freedom she was charged and found guilty of taking part in a plot to kill Elizabeth, and Mary was beheaded at Fotheringhay Castle in 1587, aged 44.

Seminar 4 — England in the 17th Century

Central concept: civil war, republic, restoration and the constitutional settlement of the 17th century (1600–1698).

  • Political timeline: from James I to the Glorious Revolution
    • 1600 East India Company founded; 1601 Poor Law passed (people made to pay a rate to support the poor); 1603 Queen Elizabeth dies and James I becomes king.
    • 1605 the Gunpowder Plot, a Catholic conspiracy to blow up parliament, is discovered; 1607 Jamestown, the first successful British colony in North America, is founded; 1611 the King James Bible is published.
    • 1625 James I dies and Charles I becomes king; 1628 William Harvey publishes his discovery of the circulation of the blood, the Petition of Right is presented to the king by parliament, and George Villiers, Duke of Buckingham, is assassinated in Portsmouth.
    • 1629–1640 the “Eleven Years Tyranny”: Charles I rules without parliament; 1632 Christopher Wren is born; 1633 William Laud becomes Archbishop of Canterbury.
    • 1641 MPs draw up a list of grievances called the Grand Remonstrance; 1642 civil war between king and parliament begins with the indecisive battle of Edgehill, and Isaac Newton is born.
    • 1644 parliament wins the battle of Marston Moor; 1645 parliament wins the battle of Naseby and William Laud is executed; 1646 Charles I surrenders to the Scots and the first civil war ends.
    • 1648 Charles I starts another civil war and the Scots intervene on his behalf, but the battle of Preston ends hopes of restoring him; Pride’s Purge — Thomas Pride removes some Presbyterian MPs from parliament; 1649 King Charles I is beheaded.
    • 1651 a Scottish army invades England to put Charles II on the throne, but the Scots are defeated at Worcester and Charles flees abroad; Thomas Hobbes publishes Leviathan; 1652 Inigo Jones dies; 1652–1654 the first Anglo-Dutch war.
    • 1653 Oliver Cromwell becomes Lord Protector; 1655–1657 the Rule of the Major-Generals; 1658 Oliver Cromwell dies and his son Richard takes over; 1659 Richard Cromwell resigns — his fall was so swift he became known as “Tumbledown Dick”.
    • 1660 Charles II becomes king; 1661 Robert Boyle publishes The Sceptical Chemist; 1662 the Royal Society is given its charter by Charles II, Charles II marries the Portuguese princess Catherine of Braganza, and the Act of Uniformity is passed.
    • 1663 the first turnpike road is opened (turnpike roads were owned by turnpike trusts that maintained them, and you had to pay to use them); 1665 plague in London — the last outbreak of bubonic plague in England; 1665–1667 the second Anglo-Dutch war.
    • 1666 the Great Fire of London destroys most of the city, but it is soon rebuilt; 1667 John Milton publishes Paradise Lost; 1670 the Hudson’s Bay Company is formed; 1672–1674 the 3rd Anglo-Dutch war.
    • 1673 the Test Act is passed — Catholics and Protestant dissenters are prevented from holding public office; 1670 the Ashmolean Museum in Oxford is founded.
    • 1678 Titus Oates claims he has discovered a “Popish plot” against Charles II (the whole thing is a hoax); 1679 the Act of Habeas Corpus outlaws imprisonment without trial.
    • 1685 Charles II dies and James II, a Roman Catholic, becomes king; the Duke of Monmouth (Charles II’s illegitimate son) leads an unsuccessful rebellion in south-west England; 1686 “Hanging” Judge Jefferys sentences many of the rebels to death.
    • 1687 Isaac Newton publishes Principia Mathematica, laying the foundations of modern physics; 1688 the “Glorious, Bloodless Revolution” — James II flees abroad and William and Mary become the new monarchs; 1689 the Bill of Rights is passed.
    • 1694 Mary dies of smallpox aged 32 and the Bank of England is founded; 1698 Thomas Savery invents the first steam engine.
  • Life in the 17th century: population, trade and colonies
    • During the 17th century the population of England and Wales grew steadily — about 4 million in 1600 and about 5.5 million by 1700; England became steadily richer, trade and commerce grew and grew, and by the late 17th century trade was an increasingly important part of the English economy.
    • Industries such as glass, brick making, iron and coal mining expanded rapidly; the port of London grew rapidly and by the late 17th century London had a population of more than half a million; towns like Liverpool, well placed to trade with North America and the West Indies, also grew rapidly.
    • England began to found colonies in North America and the West Indies: 1607 the first permanent colony at Jamestown, Virginia; 1609 settlement in Bermuda; 1620 the Mayflower sailed from Plymouth to Massachusetts; 1629 settlement in the Bahamas; 1655 Jamaica taken from the Spaniards; 1664 New York and New Jersey taken from the Dutch.
    • The East India Company was founded in 1600, and the English founded a trading post at Canton, China in 1637.
    • Banking developed: as England grew more commercial, lending money became more important; in the early 17th century goldsmiths lent and changed money; then in 1640 King Charles I confiscated gold which London merchants had deposited at the mint for safety, after which people began to deposit money with goldsmiths instead; the goldsmiths gave receipts for the gold in the form of notes promising to pay on demand; the Bank of England was founded in 1694.
    • During the 17th century the status of merchants improved, because people saw that trade was an increasingly important part of the country’s wealth, so merchants became more respected; however political power and influence was still held by rich landowners.
  • Life in the 17th century: social structure, poor relief and London
    • At the top of society were the nobility; below them the gentry — gentlemen were not quite rich but they were certainly well off; below them were yeomen, farmers who owned their own land (comfortably off but often working alongside their men, whereas gentlemen did not do manual work); below them came the mass of the population — craftsmen, tenant farmers and labourers.
    • By an act of 1601 overseers of the poor were appointed by each parish; they had power to force people to pay a local tax to help the poor; those who could not work, such as the old and disabled, would be provided for; the overseers were meant to provide work for the able-bodied poor; anyone who refused to work was whipped and, after 1610, could be placed in a house of correction; pauper’s children were sent to local employers to be apprentices.
    • In 1600 Westminster was separate from London; however in the early 17th century rich people built houses along the Thames between the two, and in the late 17th century many grand houses were built west of London while working-class houses were built east of the city — so as early as the 17th century London was divided into the affluent West End and the poor East End.
    • Stuart towns were dirty and unsanitary: people threw dirty water and other rubbish in the streets, and the streets were very narrow; at night they were dark and dangerous.
    • However there were some improvements in London: in the early 17th century a piped water supply was created, with water from a reservoir travelling along elm pipes through the streets and then along lead pipes to individual houses — but you had to pay to be connected and it was not cheap.
    • In the 1680s the streets of London were lit for the first time: an oil lamp was hung outside every tenth house and was lit for part of the year; the oil lamps did not give much light but they were better than nothing at all.
    • During the 17th century towns grew much larger despite outbreaks of plague: fleas that lived on rats transmitted bubonic plague, and if the fleas bit humans they were likely to fall victim; unfortunately at the time nobody knew what caused the plague and nobody had any idea how to treat it.
    • Plague broke out in London in 1603, 1636 and 1665; each time it killed a significant part of the population, but each time London recovered, as there were always plenty of poor people in the countryside willing to come and work in the town; other towns were also periodically devastated by the plague, but the plague of 1665 was the last — we are not certain why.
  • Life in the 17th century: housing, furniture, food and clothes
    • In the late 17th century furniture for the wealthy became more comfortable and much more finely decorated; in the early 17th century furniture was plain and heavy and usually made of oak, while in the late 17th century it was often made of walnut or (from the 1680s) mahogany and decorated in new ways.
    • One new decoration was veneering (thin pieces of expensive wood laid over cheaper wood); some furniture was also inlaid — wood was carved out and the hollow filled in with mother of pearl; at this time lacquering arrived in England, with pieces of furniture coated with lacquer in bright colours.
    • New types of furniture were introduced: in the mid-17th century chests of drawers became common, grandfather clocks also became popular, and later in the century the bookcase was introduced; chairs became far more comfortable, with upholstered (padded and covered) chairs becoming common in wealthy people’s homes, and in the 1680s the first real armchairs appeared.
    • In the early 17th century the architect Inigo Jones introduced the classical style of architecture (based on ancient Greek and Roman styles); he designed the Banqueting Hall in Whitehall, the first purely classical building in England; the late 17th century was a great age of building grand country homes, displaying the wealth of the upper class.
    • All the improvements in furniture did not apply to the poor: their furniture remained very plain and basic; however there were some improvements in poor people’s houses — by the late 17th century even poor people usually lived in houses made of brick or stone, a big improvement over wooden houses, as they were warmer and drier.
    • During the 17th century chimneys became more common and by the late 17th century even the poor had them; in 1600 glass windows were a luxury and poor people made do with linen soaked in linseed oil, but during the 17th century glass became cheaper and by the late 17th century even the poor had glass windows; in the early 17th century there were only casement windows (opening on hinges), while in the later 17th century sash windows were introduced — in two sections that slid up and down vertically to open and shut.
    • Although poor people’s homes improved in some ways they remained very small and crowded: most of the poor lived in huts of 2 or 3 rooms, and some families lived in just one room.
    • In the early 17th century people began eating with forks for the first time; during the century new foods were introduced into England (for the rich) such as bananas and pineapples, and new drinks tea and coffee; in the late 17th century there were many coffee houses in the towns where merchants and professional men met to read newspapers and talk shop.
    • In the late 17th century the rich began eating ice cream, and many rich people built special underground chambers in the grounds of their houses for preserving ice during the summer (the ice was covered in straw to preserve it).
    • For the poor, food remained plain and monotonous: they subsisted on food like bread, cheese and onions; ordinary people also ate pottage each day — a kind of stew made by boiling grain in water to make a kind of porridge, to which you added vegetables and (if you could afford it) pieces of meat or fish.
    • Clothes: at the beginning of the 17th century men wore starched collars called ruffs and women wore frames made of wood or whalebone under their dresses; however the farthingale was soon discarded and the ruff evolved into a large lace collar (for those who could afford it).
    • In the 17th century men wore knee-length, trouser-like garments called breeches, stockings and boots, and linen shirts; in the early 17th century they wore a kind of jacket called a doublet with a cape on top, and men wore their hair long and wore beards; in the late 17th century a man’s doublet became a waistcoat and men wore a frock coat over it — with breeches it was rather like a three-piece suit; men were now clean shaven and wore wigs.
    • Women wore a linen nightie-like garment called a shift, over which they wore long dresses in two parts — the bodice and the skirt; sometimes women wore two skirts, with the upper skirt gathered up to reveal an underskirt; women in the 17th century did not wear knickers; from the mid-17th century it was fashionable for women to wear black patches on their faces such as little stars or crescent moons.
  • Life in the 17th century: pastimes, education and the Scientific Revolution
    • In the 17th century traditional pastimes such as cards and bowls continued, as did games like tennis and shuttlecock; people also played board games like chess, draughts, backgammon and fox and goose.
    • The wealthy also played a game called pale-maille (Pall Mall in London gets its name from an area where the game was played), and Charles II made yachting a popular sport.
    • The theatre remained popular, but the Puritans disapproved of it and in 1642 banned it completely; theatre began again in 1660; in the early 17th century the stage jutted out into the audience, while in the late 17th century it took on its modern form; in the early 17th century boys played women’s parts, but after 1660 actresses performed.
    • Among the poor, cruel “sports” like cock fighting and bull and bear baiting were popular (a bear or bull was chained to a post and dogs were trained to attack it); the first English newspaper was printed in 1621.
    • Education: in well-off families both boys and girls went to a form of infant school called a petty school, but only boys went to grammar school; upper-class girls (and sometimes boys) were taught by tutors, and middle-class girls might be taught by their mothers; during the 17th century boarding schools for girls were founded in many towns, where girls were taught subjects like writing, music and needlework (it was considered more important for girls to learn “accomplishments” than to study academic subjects).
    • In the grammar schools conditions were hard: boys started work at 6 or 7 in the morning and worked to 5 or 5.30 pm with breaks for meals; corporal punishment was usual — normally the teacher hit naughty boys on the bare buttocks with birch twigs, while other boys in the class would hold the naughty boy down.
    • A revolution in thought occurred during the 17th century: the ancient Greeks could be said to be scientists, as they thought that by using their reason they could work out why the natural world behaves as it does, but they never tested their theories by carrying out practical experiments, so many of their ideas were wrong.
    • Unfortunately the ancient Greek philosophers were held in very high esteem and for centuries hardly anyone questioned their theories; this began to change in the late 16th and early 17th centuries, when people began to conduct experiments to see if theories about the world were true.
    • In England Francis Bacon (1561–1626) declared that people should not accept that a theory was true just because a Greek philosopher said it was, and argued that careful observation and experiment was the key to finding out how the natural world works; gradually this new method of understanding the world took over, and by the late 17th century the new scientific approach had triumphed everywhere in Europe.
    • There was also a change in attitude: previously people saw nature almost as a living thing, but now they began to see it as a machine (Isaac Newton called God the “Divine Watchmaker”); Francis Bacon believed that science (or natural philosophy, as it was called) could greatly improve people’s lives, and by the late 17th century there was a new mood of optimism.
    • In 1645 a group of philosophers and mathematicians began holding meetings to discuss science; Charles II was interested in science and in 1662 made the club the Royal Society.
    • The arts also flourished in late 17th-century England: the architect Christopher Wren (1632–1723) designed many buildings including St Paul’s Cathedral; the poet John Milton (1608–1674) wrote his masterpiece Paradise Lost; and it was the era of the composer Henry Purcell (1659–1695).
    • During the 17th century belief in witchcraft and magic also declined; the last person to be executed for witchcraft in England met her death in 1685 (witches were hanged in England, not burned).
  • Documents: Petition of Right, Habeas Corpus Act, Bill of Rights
    • The Petition of Right (1628) is a parliamentary declaration of the rights and liberties of the people, assented to by Charles I at the beginning of his reign; it became one of the three constitutional documents of English civil liberties, along with Magna Carta and the Bill of Rights 1689.
    • Refusal by Parliament to finance the king’s unpopular foreign policy had caused his government to exact forced loans and to quarter troops in subjects’ houses as an economy measure; arbitrary arrest and imprisonment for opposing these policies produced in Parliament a violent hostility to Charles and to the Duke of Buckingham.
    • The Petition was initiated by Sir Edward Coke, an English barrister, judge and later opposition politician; it was based upon earlier statutes and charters and asserted four principles: no taxes may be levied without consent of Parliament; no subject may be imprisoned without cause shown (a reaffirmation of the right of habeas corpus); no soldiers may be quartered upon the citizenry; and martial law may not be used in time of peace.
    • In return for his acceptance in June 1628, Charles was granted subsidies; although the petition was important as a safeguard of civil liberties, its spirit was soon violated by Charles, who continued to collect tonnage and poundage duties without Parliament’s authorization and to prosecute citizens in an arbitrary manner; this struggle resulted in a civil war and ended with the beheading of Charles I in 1649.
    • The Habeas Corpus Act of 1679 was passed during the reign of the restored monarchy of Charles II after the English Civil War; it strengthened the ancient and powerful writ which had been a feature of English Common Law since before Magna Carta, and formalized the recognition of the right of an individual to challenge imprisonment as unlawful.
    • Habeas Corpus is Latin for “you may have the body”, which means subject to legal examination before a court or a judge; the creation of the Act is considered a vitally important moment in legal history, as it paved the way for individual rights.
    • The Act covered only criminal charges: it guaranteed that a person detained by the authorities would have to be brought before a court of law and produce the reason for his detention so that the legality of the detention could be established.
    • In times of social unrest, however, Parliament had the power to suspend habeas corpus; the government considered suspending it as early as November and December 1792 following reports of radical meetings and rioting in the Midlands, East Anglia, the north-east and Scotland, as well as the French legislature’s “fraternal” decree offering aid to all peoples seeking to throw off the chains of tyranny; the Suspension of Habeas Corpus Act was passed on 7 May 1794 and habeas corpus was suspended on 16 May 1794, lasting from May 1794 to July 1795 and again from April 1798 to March 1801.
    • The practical effect of the suspension was that the authorities could arrest anyone on suspicion of having committed a crime and detain them indefinitely without bringing specific charges.
    • Further Habeas Corpus Acts were passed in 1803, 1804, 1816 and 1862, but it is the Act of 1679 which is remembered as one of the most important statutes in English constitutional history; though amended, it remains on the statute book to this day and is stored in the Parliamentary Archives.
    • Key clauses: no person delivered or set at large upon any Habeas Corpus shall at any time hereafter be again imprisoned or committed for the same offence by any person whatsoever, other than by the legal order and process of such court; if any other person knowingly recommits or imprisons, or knowingly procures the recommitment or imprisonment, for the same offence, he shall forfeit to the prisoner or party grieved the sum of £500; no person shall be sued or molested for any offence against this Act unless the party offending be sued within two years (or, if the party grieved is in prison, within two years after the decease or delivery of the imprisoned person); after the Assizes are proclaimed, no person shall be removed from the common gaol under any Habeas Corpus granted in pursuance of this Act, but shall be brought before the Judge of Assizes in open court; and if any information, suit or action is brought against any person for an offence against this law, the defendant may plead the general issue that they are not guilty, or that they owe nothing.
    • The Bill of Rights 1689 is an act of the Parliament of England whose title is “An Act Declaring the Rights and Liberties of the Subject and Settling the Succession of the Crown”; it was passed by Parliament on 16 December 1689.
    • The main principles of the Bill of Rights are still in force today — particularly cited in legal cases — and it was used as a model for the US Bill of Rights 1789; its influence can also be seen in other documents establishing the rights of humans, such as the United Nations Declaration of Human Rights and the European Convention on Human Rights.
    • The Bill of Rights represented the end of the concept of the Divine Right of Kings, which was one of the issues over which the English Civil War had been fought; it also made kings and queens subject to laws passed by Parliament.
    • In the UK, the Bill of Rights is further accompanied by Magna Carta, the Habeas Corpus Act 1679 and the Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949 as some of the basic documents of the uncodified British constitution.
    • In addition to listing the transgressions of James II, the Act legislated that Roman Catholics were barred from the throne; that it is the right of the subjects to petition the king and all commitments and prosecutions for such petitioning are illegal; that the sovereign may not arbitrarily suspend Parliament’s laws; that the sovereign’s right to raise money through taxation is limited, with the consent of Parliament being needed; that election of members of Parliament ought to be free; that the freedom of speech and debates or proceedings in Parliament ought not to be impeached or questioned in any court or place out of Parliament; that it is illegal for the sovereign to keep military forces within the kingdom in time of peace without consent of Parliament (one of the reasons the military is funded on an annual basis by parliament); and that the imposition of excessive fines or cruel punishments is illegal.
    • The Bill of Rights was one of three very important laws made at this time; the other two were the 1689 Toleration Act (which promoted religious toleration) and the 1694 Triennial Act, which prevented the King from dissolving Parliament at his will and held that general elections had to be held every three years.
    • Declarations of the Bill: the pretended power of suspending the laws or the execution of laws by regal authority without consent of Parliament is illegal; the pretended power of dispensing with laws or the execution of laws by regal authority, as it hath been assumed and exercised of late, is illegal; the commission for erecting the late Court of Commissioners for Ecclesiastical Causes, and all other commissions and courts of like nature, are illegal and pernicious; levying money for or to the use of the Crown by pretence of prerogative, without grant of Parliament, for longer time or in other manner than the same is or shall be granted, is illegal; it is the right of the subjects to petition the king; the raising or keeping a standing army within the kingdom in time of peace, unless it be with consent of Parliament, is against law; the subjects which are Protestants may have arms for their defence suitable to their conditions and as allowed by law; election of members of Parliament ought to be free; freedom of speech and debates in Parliament ought not to be impeached or questioned outside Parliament; excessive bail ought not to be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor cruel and unusual punishments inflicted; jurors ought to be duly impanelled and returned, and jurors which pass upon men in trials for high treason ought to be freeholders; all grants and promises of fines and forfeitures of particular persons before conviction are illegal and void; and for redress of all grievances Parliaments ought to be held frequently.
    • Finally, the Lords Spiritual and Temporal and Commons resolved that William and Mary, prince and princess of Orange, be declared king and queen of England, France and Ireland and the dominions thereunto belonging, to hold the crown and royal dignity during their lives and the life of the survivor, with the sole and full exercise of the regal power only in and executed by the said prince of Orange in the names of the said prince and princess during their joint lives; after their deceases the crown was to pass to the heirs of the body of the said princess, and for default of such issue to the Princess Anne of Denmark and the heirs of her body, and for default of such issue to the heirs of the body of the said prince of Orange.
  • Historic figure: Oliver Cromwell
    • Quote: “I was by birth a gentleman, living neither in any considerable height, nor yet in obscurity. I have been called to several employments in the nation — to serve in parliaments, and (because I would not be over tedious) I did endeavour to discharge the duty of an honest man in those services, to God, and his people’s interest, and of the commonwealth; having, when time was, a competent acceptation in the hearts of men, and some evidence thereof.”
    • Oliver Cromwell was born into a family which was for a time one of the wealthiest and most influential in the area; educated at Huntingdon grammar school (now the Cromwell Museum) and at Cambridge University, he became a minor East Anglian landowner.
    • He made a living by farming and collecting rents — first in his native Huntingdon, then from 1631 in St Ives and from 1636 in Ely; Cromwell’s inheritances from his father (who died in 1617) and later from a maternal uncle were not great, his income was modest and he had to support an expanding family: widowed mother, wife and eight children.
    • He ranked near the bottom of the landed elite, the landowning class often labelled “the gentry” which dominated the social and political life of the county; until 1640 he played only a small role in local administration and no significant role in national politics — it was the civil wars of the 1640s which lifted Cromwell from obscurity to power.
    • From the outbreak of war in summer 1642 Cromwell was an active and committed officer in the parliamentary army; initially a captain in charge of a small body of mounted troops, in 1643 he was promoted to colonel and given command of his own cavalry regiment.
    • He was successful in a series of sieges and small battles which helped to secure East Anglia and the East Midlands against the royalists; at the end of the year he was appointed second in command of the Eastern Association army, parliament’s largest and most effective regional army, with the rank of lieutenant-general.
    • During 1644 he contributed to the victory at Marston Moor, which helped secure the north for parliament, and also campaigned with mixed results in the south Midlands and Home Counties.
    • In 1645–1646, as second in command of the newly formed main parliamentary army, the New Model Army, Cromwell played a major role in parliament’s victory in the Midlands, sealed by the battle of Naseby in June 1645, and in the south and south-west.
    • When civil war flared up again in 1648 he commanded a large part of the New Model Army which first crushed rebellion in South Wales and then at Preston defeated a Scottish-royalist army of invasion.
    • After the trial and execution of the King, Cromwell led major military campaigns to establish English control over Ireland (1649–1650) and then Scotland (1650–1651), culminating in the defeat of another Scottish-royalist army of invasion at Worcester in September 1651; in summer 1650, before embarking for Scotland, Cromwell had been appointed lord general — that is, commander in chief — of all the parliamentary forces.
    • It was a remarkable achievement for a man who probably had no military experience before 1642; Cromwell consistently attributed his military success to God’s will, while historians point to his personal courage and skill, to his care in training and equipping his men, and to the tight discipline he imposed both on and off the battlefield.
    • Cromwell’s military standing gave him enhanced political power, just as his military victories gave him the confidence and motivation to intervene in and to shape political events; an obscure and inexperienced MP for Cambridge in 1640, by the late 1640s he was one of the power-brokers in parliament and played a decisive role in the “revolution” of winter 1648–1649 which saw the trial and execution of the King and the abolition of monarchy and the House of Lords.
    • As head of the army he intervened several times to support or remove the republican regimes of the early 1650s; eventually, in December 1653, he became head of state as Lord Protector, though he held that office under a written constitution which ensured that he would share political power with parliaments and a council.
    • As Lord Protector for almost five years, until his death on 3 September 1658, Cromwell was able to mould policies and to fulfil some of his goals; he headed a tolerant, inclusive and largely civilian regime, which sought to restore order and stability at home and thus to win over much of the traditional political and social elite; abroad, the army and navy were employed to promote England’s interests in an expansive and largely successful foreign policy.

Seminar 5 — England in the 18th & 19th Centuries

Central concept: from the Hanoverian succession and the Industrial Revolution to the Victorian age (1701–1901).

  • 18th-century timeline: succession, war and empire
    • 1701 the Act of Settlement is passed and Jethro Tull invents the seed drill; 1702 William dies and Anne becomes queen.
    • 1704 the Duke of Marlborough defeats the French at the Battle of Blenheim and the British capture Gibraltar; 1706 Marlborough defeats the French army at Ramillies; 1707 the Act of Union joins England and Scotland; 1708 Marlborough defeats the army of Louis XIV at Oudenarde; 1709 Marlborough wins the battle of Malplaquet against the French.
    • 1714 Queen Anne dies and George I becomes king; 1715 the first Jacobite uprising — the Highlanders rise but the uprising ends in an indecisive battle near Stirling.
    • 1745 the second Jacobite uprising — the Jacobites invade England and reach as far as Derby but then turn back; 1746 the Jacobites are crushed at the battle of Culloden.
    • 1756 the Seven Years War against France begins; 1759 General Wolfe captures Quebec but is killed — his victory ensures Canada will be a British colony rather than a French one; 1763 the Seven Years War ends.
    • 1783 Britain signs a treaty recognising the independence of the American colonies.
  • 18th-century timeline: industry, invention and society
    • 1709 Abraham Darby uses coke instead of charcoal to smelt iron; 1712 Newcomen makes steam engines for pumping water out of mines; 1733 John Kay invents the flying shuttle.
    • 1761 the Bridgwater canal opens; 1769 James Watt patents an improved steam engine; 1771 Richard Arkwright introduces a loom powered by a water mill; 1779 the world’s first iron bridge is built in Shropshire and Samuel Crompton invents the spinning mule.
    • c. 1780 the Industrial Revolution begins to transform Britain; 1784 Henry Cort invents the “puddling” process, a new way of making wrought iron, and as a result iron production booms; 1785 Edmund Cartwright invents the power loom (one worked by a steam engine) and cotton production grows very rapidly.
    • 1792 gas light is invented; 1796 Jenner invents vaccination against smallpox; 1799 income tax is introduced to pay for the war against France.
    • 1719 Daniel Defoe publishes Robinson Crusoe; 1720 the South Sea Bubble (stocks in the South Sea Company suddenly fall in price and many people lose huge sums); 1721 Robert Walpole becomes the king’s main minister and people call him the Prime Minister (originally a term of abuse).
    • 1723 Christopher Wren dies; 1727 George I dies, George II becomes king and Isaac Newton dies; 1735 the Prime Minister moves into 10 Downing Street; 1739 the highwayman Dick Turpin is hanged and John Wesley founds the Methodists; 1742 Robert Walpole resigns.
    • 1773 the Stock Exchange is founded; 1775 Jane Austen is born; c. 1780 Lord George Gordon leads anti-Catholic riots in London; 1787 the first convicts leave Britain from Portsmouth for Australia; 1788 Lord Byron is born.
  • 18th-century life: society, towns, housing and clothes
    • In the mid-18th century the population of Britain was about 6.5 million; in the late 18th century it grew rapidly and by 1801 it was over 9 million.
    • Owning land was the main form of wealth in the 18th century and political power and influence was in the hands of rich landowners; at the top were the nobility, below them a class of nearly rich landowners called the gentry; in the early 18th century there was another class of landowners called yeomen between the rich and the poor, but during the century this class became less and less numerous.
    • However other middle-class people such as merchants and professional men became richer and more numerous, especially in the towns; below them were the great mass of the population, craftsmen and labourers; in the 18th century probably half the population lived at subsistence or bare survival level.
    • During the 18th century towns grew larger; nevertheless most towns still had populations of less than 10,000, but in the late 18th century new industrial towns in the Midlands and the north of England mushroomed, while the population of London grew to nearly 1 million by the end of the century.
    • Many towns were improved in the later 18th century when bodies of men called Paving or Improvement Commissioners were formed by Acts of Parliament; they had powers to pave and clean the streets and sometimes to light them with oil lamps, and some also arranged collections of rubbish — since most of it was organic it could be sold as fertiliser.
    • A small minority of the population lived in luxury: the rich built great country houses, and a famous landscape gardener called Lancelot Brown (1715–1783) created beautiful gardens (he was known as “Capability” Brown from his habit of looking at land and saying it had “great capabilities”); the leading architect of the 18th century was Robert Adam (1728–1792), who created a style called neo-classical and designed many 18th-century country houses.
    • The wealthy owned comfortable upholstered furniture and beautiful furniture, some of it veneered or inlaid; however the poor had none of these things — craftsmen and labourers lived in 2 or 3 rooms, the poorest people lived in just one room, and their furniture was very simple and plain.
    • Clothes: in the 18th century men wore knee-length trouser-like garments called breeches and stockings, and also waistcoats and frock coats, with linen shirts; both men and women wore wigs, and for men three-cornered hats were popular, along with buckled shoes.
    • Women wore stays (a bodice with strips of whalebone) and hooped petticoats under their dresses; women in the 18th century did not wear knickers; fashionable women carried folding fans; fashion was very important for the wealthy, but poor people’s clothes hardly changed at all.
  • 18th-century life: pastimes and education
    • Traditional games remained popular, including chess, draughts and backgammon; people also played dominoes and tennis and a rough version of football.
    • Horse racing had been carried on for centuries, but at this time it became a professional sport: the Jockey Club was formed in 1727 and the Derby began in 1780.
    • For the well off, card games and gambling were popular, and the theatre was also popular; in the early 18th century most towns did not have a purpose-built theatre and plays were staged in buildings like inns, but in the late 18th century theatres were built in most towns.
    • Assembly rooms were also built in most towns, where people played cards and attended balls; in London pleasure gardens were created.
    • A kind of cricket was played long before the 18th century, but at that time it took on its modern form; the first cricket club was formed at Hambledon in Hampshire about 1750.
    • Also in the 18th century rich people visited spas, believing that bathing in and/or drinking spa water could cure illness — towns like Buxton, Bath and Tunbridge prospered; at the end of the 18th century wealthy people began to spend time at the seaside (again believing that bathing in seawater was good for your health), and seaside resorts like Brighton and Bognor boomed.
    • Reading was also a popular pastime and the first novels were published at this time; books were still expensive but in many towns you could pay to join a circulating library; the first daily newspaper in England was printed in 1702 and The Times began in 1785.
    • Many people enjoyed cruel “sports” like cockfighting and bull baiting (a bull was chained to a post and dogs were trained to attack it); rich people liked fox hunting; public executions were also popular and drew large crowds; boxing without gloves was popular (although some boxers began to wear leather gloves in the 18th century); puppet shows like Punch and Judy also drew the crowds.
    • Furthermore in the late 18th century the circus became a popular form of entertainment; smoking clay pipes was popular in the 18th century, so was taking snuff; wealthy young men would go on a “grand tour” of Europe lasting one or two years.
    • Education: in the early 18th century charity schools were founded in many towns — they were sometimes called Blue Coat Schools because of the colour of the children’s uniforms; boys from well-off families went to grammar schools, and girls from well-off families also went to school, but it was felt important for them to learn “accomplishments” like embroidery and music rather than academic subjects.
    • However nonconformists or dissenters (Protestants who did not belong to the Church of England) were not allowed to attend most public schools; instead they went to their own dissenting academies.
  • 18th-century documents: Act of Settlement (1701) and Act of Union (1707)
    • The Act of Settlement of 1701 was designed to secure the Protestant succession to the throne and to strengthen the guarantees for ensuring the parliamentary system of government; it also strengthened the Bill of Rights (1689).
    • Although the Bill of Rights had established the order of succession with the heirs of Mary II, Anne and William III, neither of James II’s daughters had surviving heirs, casting uncertainty on the future of the succession: Mary had died of smallpox in 1694 aged 32, by 1700 William was dying, and Anne’s only surviving child (out of 17 children), the Duke of Gloucester, had died that same year at the age of 11.
    • Without a confirmed heir the decision was made by parliament to ensure that the succession of future sovereigns remained within the Protestant faith; according to the 1701 Act, succession went to Princess Sophia, Electress of Hanover (James I’s granddaughter) and her Protestant heirs.
    • However Sophia died before Queen Anne, so the succession passed to her son George, Elector of Hanover; this Act brought the Hanoverian dynasty to the throne in the person of George I in 1714 and kept it there until the accession of Victoria in 1837; the Act was later extended to Scotland as a result of the Treaty of Union enacted in the Acts of Union of 1707.
    • The Act laid down the conditions under which alone the Crown could be held: no Roman Catholic, nor anyone married to a Roman Catholic, could hold the English Crown; the Sovereign now had to swear to maintain the Church of England (and after 1707, the Church of Scotland).
    • It also further restricted the powers and prerogatives of the Crown: the sovereign must be not only a Protestant but a member of the Church of England; he must not leave the country without consent of parliament (repealed in the reign of George I); England was not to be involved in war for the defence of foreign territories; only English-born subjects could be admitted to parliament, to public offices civil or military, or to the Privy Council (by the Naturalization Act of 1870 this clause was virtually repealed for all persons who obtain a certificate of naturalization); judges were to hold office during good behaviour rather than at the sovereign’s pleasure, though subject to impeachment by both houses of Parliament; and impeachments by the House of Commons are not subject to pardon under the Great Seal of England.
    • The act as originally passed contained other clauses: all matters properly cognizable in the Privy Council were to be transacted there, and all resolutions were to be signed by such of the Privy Council as should advise and consent to the same; and all officeholders and pensioners under the Crown were incapable of sitting in the House of Commons; the first of these clauses, an attempt to destroy the growing power of the Cabinet, was repealed, and the second was seriously modified in 1706.
    • The Act of Union (1707) is the treaty that effected the union of England and Scotland under the name of Great Britain; since 1603 England and Scotland had been under the same monarchs.
    • The two kingdoms were to be united, the Protestant succession was to be adopted, and trade was to be free and equal throughout Great Britain and its dominions; subject to certain temporary concessions, taxation direct and indirect would also be uniform, and England compensated Scotland for undertaking to share responsibility for England’s national debt by payment of an equivalent of £398,085 10 shillings.
    • Simply put, the Scots needed financial support from England, and the English wanted to ensure that Scotland would not choose a different monarch; it was not, however, a universally popular move, and many troubles had to be overcome as the two different sets of traditions and practice were merged into one parliament; the 1707 Union of Parliaments would open up the English colonial markets to Scottish trade.
    • Queen Anne had recommended the union of the two kingdoms in her first speech to parliament in 1702; with only minor amendments the Scottish Parliament passed the treaty on 16 January 1707, on 28 February the Commons in England followed suit, and Queen Anne gave the royal assent on 6 March, bringing into effect the Treaty of Union with its 25 Articles on 1 May 1707.
    • On this date the Scottish Parliament and the English Parliament united to form the Parliament of Great Britain, based in the Palace of Westminster in London, the home of the English Parliament; monetary union and a customs union were created; Scotland was allowed to have its own established (Presbyterian) church; Scottish law would remain in force and the Court of Session would decide it; provisions of the Act of Settlement 1701 were confirmed, so “that all Papists and persons marrying Papists shall be excluded” from the throne; and a new Union flag combined the Cross of St Andrew and the Cross of St George.
    • Neither side was completely happy with the Union: the Scottish people in particular had to balance the loss of their ancient independence against the need to open themselves up to a wider world and greater opportunities than their own country could provide, while the English gained needed security, for no longer could European powers use Scotland as a base for an attack on its southern neighbour.
    • Key articles: Article 1 — the two kingdoms shall be united into one kingdom by the name of Great Britain; Article 3 — the United Kingdom shall be represented by one and the same Parliament, styled the Parliament of Great Britain; Article 4 — full freedom and intercourse of trade and navigation to and from any port or place within the United Kingdom and the dominions and plantations; Article 6 — all parts of the United Kingdom shall have the same allowances, encouragements and drawbacks and be under the same prohibitions, restrictions and regulations of trade, and liable to the same customs and duties on import and export.
    • Article 8 — all foreign salt imported into Scotland shall be charged with the same duties as the like salt imported into England; Article 9 — whenever the sum of £1,997,763 8s 4d is enacted to be raised in England on land and other things usually charged, Scotland shall be charged with a further sum of £48,000 as the quota of Scotland; Article 16 — the coin shall be of the same standard and value throughout the United Kingdom as now in England, and a mint shall be continued in Scotland under the same rules; Article 17 — the same weights and measures shall be used throughout the United Kingdom as are now established in England; Article 21 — the rights and privileges of the Royal Burghs in Scotland shall remain entire after the Union; Article 23 — the 16 Peers of Scotland shall have all the privileges of Parliament which the Peers of England now have.
  • 19th-century timeline: reform, industry and science
    • 1801 the first census is held and another Act of Union joins Ireland to England and Scotland; 1805 the battle of Trafalgar; 1807 the slave trade is abolished; 1809 Charles Darwin is born.
    • 1811 Prince George becomes Prince Regent as his father is insane; 1812 the Prime Minister Spencer Perceval is shot by John Bellingham and Charles Dickens is born; 1813 the Duke of Wellington defeats the French army at the battle of Vitoria in Spain.
    • 1815 the battle of Waterloo and Humphrey Davy invents the miners’ safety lamp, which saves many lives; 1817 Jane Austen dies; 1818 Queen Charlotte dies.
    • 1819 the Peterloo Massacre — cavalry charge unarmed people holding a meeting on St Peter’s Fields at Manchester, and eleven people are killed; Sir Stamford Raffles founds Singapore; 1820 George III dies and George IV becomes king.
    • 1821 John Constable paints The Haywain; 1824 Lord Byron dies; 1825 the world’s first public passenger railway opens (the Stockton and Darlington railway).
    • 1829 the Catholic Emancipation Act gives Catholics civil rights and Sir Robert Peel forms the first modern police force (hence their nicknames “bobbies” or “peelers”); 1830 George IV dies and William IV becomes king.
    • 1832 the Great Reform Act is passed — seats in parliament are distributed more fairly and the middle class are given the vote; the writer Lewis Carroll is born; 1833 slavery is abolished throughout the British Empire.
    • 1835 bull baiting is banned (chaining a bull to a post then using trained dogs to attack it); 1837 William IV dies and Victoria becomes queen.
    • 1840 the penny black stamp is introduced, the writer Thomas Hardy is born, and Queen Victoria marries Albert; 1842 a new law bans women and children from working underground in mines.
    • 1843 Charles Dickens publishes A Christmas Carol and the first Christmas card goes on sale; 1847 a new law bans women and children from working more than 10 hours a day, Charlotte Brontë publishes Jane Eyre and Alexander Graham Bell is born.
    • 1848 there are cholera epidemics in British towns and the Health Act is passed; 1851 the Great Exhibition is held in London; 1854–1856 the Crimean War is fought and Britain and France defeat the Russians.
    • 1856 Henry Bessemer invents a way of converting pig iron to steel; 1857–1858 the Indian Mutiny takes place; 1859 Darwin publishes The Origin of Species, outlining his theory of evolution.
    • 1860 HMS Warrior, Britain’s first iron warship, is launched; 1861 Prince Albert dies; 1863 the first (steam-driven) underground train runs in London.
    • 1865 Joseph Lister invents antiseptic surgery, the Salvation Army is formed (it does not get its name till 1878), and Lewis Carroll publishes Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland; 1867 the second Reform Act increases the number of people allowed to vote.
    • 1868 the first Trades Union Congress is held and the last public execution is carried out in England; 1869 Richard Blackmore publishes Lorna Doone; 1870 the Education Act provides state education for all and Charles Dickens dies.
    • 1871 the Bank Holiday Act is passed (for most working-class people bank holidays are their only paid holidays); 1872 the secret ballot is introduced and the second Public Health Act is passed; 1874 the writer G. K. Chesterton is born.
    • 1875 a law bans the practice of sending small boys up chimneys to clean them (from now on you have to be over 21 to clean a chimney that way) and the third Public Health Act is passed — conditions in towns and cities are slowly getting better, and in the 1870s and 1880s networks of sewers are dug and water pipes are laid; gas light becomes common even in the poorest homes; Captain Matthew Webb swims the English Channel.
    • 1877 Arthur Downsend and Thomas P. Blunt research the effect of light upon bacteria and other organisms; 1878 the Salvation Army is named; 1881 Alexander Fleming is born; 1884 the 3rd Reform Act gives more men the vote.
    • 1888 girls who make matchboxes successfully strike; 1889 gas workers and London dockworkers successfully strike — for the first time unskilled workers are forming successful trade unions; 1890 the first electric underground trains run in London; 1898 Lewis Carroll dies; 1899–1902 the Boer War is fought.
  • 19th-century life: population, class, family and religion
    • In the 19th century Britain became the world’s first industrial society and the first urban society; by 1851 more than half the population lived in towns.
    • The population boomed: in 1801 it was about 9 million and by 1901 it had risen to about 41 million — despite the fact that many people emigrated to North America and Australia to escape poverty (about 15 million left Britain between 1815 and 1914).
    • However there were also many immigrants: in the 1840s many people came from Ireland, fleeing a terrible potato famine, and in the 1880s, when the Tsar began persecuting Russian Jews, some fled to Britain and settled in the East End of London.
    • In the early 19th century Britain was an oligarchy — only a small minority of men (and no women) were allowed to vote; the situation began to change in 1832 when the vote was given to more men, constituencies were redrawn and many industrial towns were represented for the first time; the franchise was extended again in 1867 and 1884, and in 1872 the secret ballot was introduced.
    • However in the 19th century at least 80% of the population was working class; in order to be considered middle class you had to have at least one servant, and most servants were female (male servants were much more expensive because men were paid much higher wages); throughout the 19th century “service” was a major employer of women.
    • Families were much larger than today, partly because infant mortality was high: people had many children and accepted that not all of them would survive; in a 19th-century family the father was head of the family and his wife and children respected and obeyed him (at least that was the theory).
    • Until 1882 all a woman’s property, even the money she earned, belonged to her husband; divorce was made legal in 1857 but it was very rare in the 19th century.
    • Organised religion was much more important in the 19th century than it is today; nevertheless in 1851 a survey showed that only about 40% of the population were at church or chapel on a given Sunday — even allowing for those who were ill or could not make it for some other reason, it meant that half the population did not go to church; certainly many of the poor had little or no contact with the church.
    • In 1881 a similar survey showed only about a third of the population at church on a given Sunday, so in the late 19th century organised religion was in decline.
  • 19th-century life: housing, domestic technology and food
    • Well-off Victorians lived in very comfortable houses (although their servants lived in cramped quarters, often in the attic); for the first time furniture was mass-produced, which meant it was cheaper but unfortunately standards of design fell; to us, middle-class 19th-century homes would seem overcrowded with furniture, ornaments and knick-knacks — however only a small minority could afford this comfortable lifestyle.
    • In the early 19th century housing for the poor was often dreadful: they lived in “back-to-backs” — houses of three (or sometimes only two) rooms, one on top of another; the houses were literally back-to-back, with the back of one house joined onto the back of another and windows on one side only; the bottom room was used as a living room cum kitchen and the two rooms upstairs were used as bedrooms.
    • The worst homes were cellar dwellings — one-room cellars that were damp and poorly ventilated; the poorest people slept on piles of straw because they could not afford beds; fortunately in the 1840s local councils passed by-laws banning cellar dwellings and any new back-to-backs, and the old ones were gradually demolished and replaced over the following decades.
    • In the early 19th century skilled workers usually lived in “through houses”, i.e. ones that were not joined to the backs of other houses; usually they had two rooms downstairs and two upstairs; the downstairs front room was kept for best, with the family’s best furniture and ornaments, while the family spent most of their time in the downstairs back room, which served as a kitchen and living room; as the 19th century passed more and more working-class Victorians could afford this lifestyle.
    • The carpet sweeper was invented in 1876; in the late 19th century workers’ houses greatly improved — after 1875 most towns passed building regulations which stated, for example, that new houses must be a certain distance apart, rooms must be of a certain size and have windows of a certain size.
    • By the 1880s most working-class people lived in houses with two rooms downstairs and two or even three bedrooms, and most had a small garden; at the end of the 19th century some houses for skilled workers were built with the latest luxury — an indoor toilet.
    • In the late 19th century most homes also had a scullery, in which was a “copper”, a metal container for washing clothes; the copper was filled with water and soap powder was added, and to wash the clothes they were turned with a wooden tool called a dolly, or you used a metal plunger with holes in it to push clothes up and down; wet clothes were wrung through a mangle to dry them.
    • At the beginning of the 19th century people cooked over an open fire, which was very wasteful as most of the heat went up the chimney; in the 1820s an iron cooker called a grange was introduced — a much more efficient way of cooking because most of the heat was contained within; by the mid-19th century granges were common, and most of them had a boiler behind the coal fire where water was heated.
    • However even at the end of the 19th century there were still many families living in one room; old houses were sometimes divided up into separate dwellings, and sometimes if windows were broken slum landlords could not or would not replace them, so they were “repaired” with paper, or rags were stuffed into holes in the glass.
    • Lighting: gaslight first became common in well-off people’s homes in the 1840s, and by the late 1870s most working-class homes had gaslight, at least downstairs (bedrooms might have oil lamps); gas fires first became common in the 1880s and gas cookers in the 1890s.
    • Joseph Swan invented the electric light bulb in 1878 (Thomas Edison invented an improved version in 1879); the first town to have electric streetlight was Godalming in 1881, but electric light was expensive and it took a long time to replace gas in people’s homes.
    • In the early 19th century only rich people had bathrooms; people did take baths but only a few people had actual rooms for washing; in the 1870s and 1880s many middle-class Victorians had bathrooms built, with the water heated by gas, while working-class people had a tin bath and washed in front of the kitchen grange.
    • In the 1890s, for the well-to-do, a new style of art and decoration appeared called Art Nouveau, involving swirling and flowing lines and stylised plant forms.
    • Food: in the early 19th century most of the working class lived on a dreary diet of bread, butter, potatoes and bacon, and butcher’s meat was a luxury; however things greatly improved in the late 19th century — railways and steamships made it possible to import cheap grain from North America so bread became cheaper, refrigeration made it possible to import cheap meat from Argentina and Australia, and consumption of sugar also increased; by the end of the 19th century most people (not all) had a reasonably varied diet.
    • The first fish and chip shops in Britain opened in the 1860s and by the late 19th century they were common in towns and cities; in the late 19th century the first convenience foods in tins and jars went on sale — although the principle of canning was invented at the end of the 18th century, tinned food first became widely available in the 1880s.
    • Furthermore in the 1870s margarine, a cheap substitute for butter, was invented, and tomato ketchup was invented in 1874; several new biscuits were invented in the 19th century including the Garibaldi (1861), the cream cracker (1885) and the Digestive (1892), and new sweets including peanut brittle (1890) and liquorice allsorts (1899).
    • For centuries people drank chocolate, but the first chocolate bar was made in 1847 and milk chocolate was invented in 1875.
  • 19th-century life: holidays, sport, photography and education
    • In 1871 the Bank Holiday Act gave workers a few paid holidays each year; also in the 1870s some clerks and skilled workers began to have a week’s paid annual holiday, but even at the end of the 19th century most people had no paid holidays except bank holidays.
    • In the early 19th century everyone had Sunday off; in the 1870s some skilled workers began to have Saturday afternoon off; in the 1890s most workers gained a half-day holiday on Saturday and the weekend was born.
    • During the 19th century sports became organised: the London Football Association devised the rules of football in 1863, the first international match was held between England and Scotland in 1872, in 1867 John Graham Chambers drew up a list of rules for boxing (called the Queensberry Rules after the Marquis of Queensberry), and the Amateur Athletics Association was founded in 1880.
    • Several new sports and games were invented during the 19th century: William Webb Ellis is supposed to have invented Rugby at Rugby school in 1823 when he picked up a football and ran with it; lawn tennis was invented in 1873; snooker was invented in India in 1875; volleyball was invented in 1895.
    • At the end of the 19th century bicycling became a popular sport: the safety bicycle went on sale in 1885 and in 1892 John Boyd Dunlop invented pneumatic tyres (much more comfortable than solid rubber ones), and bicycling clubs became common.
    • One new hobby in the 19th century was photography: Henry Fox Talbot took the first photograph in 1835, but photography was more than just a pastime — in 1871 a writer said that one of the great comforts for the working class was having a photo of a family member who was working a long way off, so they could be reminded what their loved one looked like; the first cheap camera was invented in 1888 by George Eastman, after which photography became a popular hobby.
    • In the late 19th century town councils laid out public parks for recreation; the first children’s playground was built in a park in Manchester in 1859; and the first department store opened in London in 1863.
    • In the 19th century the “modern” Christmas evolved: before then Christmas wasn’t especially important, being one of only many festivals celebrated during the year, but the Victorians invented the Christmas card and the Christmas cracker; the Christmas tree was known in England before the 19th century but was really made popular when the royal family were shown in a magazine illustration with one; Father Christmas or Santa Claus became the figure we know today in the 19th century.
    • Education: in the early 19th century the churches provided schools for poor children, and from 1833 the government provided them with grants; there were also dame schools, run by women who taught a little reading, writing and arithmetic, although many dame schools were really a child-minding service.
    • The state did not take responsibility for education until 1870, when Forster’s Education Act laid down that schools should be provided for all children; if there were not enough places in existing schools then board schools were built.
    • In 1880 school was made compulsory for 5- to 10-year-olds, but school was not free except for the poorest children until 1891 when fees were abolished; from 1899 children were required to go to school until they were 12.
    • Girls from upper-class families were taught by a governess and boys were often sent to public schools like Eton; middle-class boys went to grammar schools and middle-class girls went to private schools where they were taught “accomplishments” such as music and sewing; discipline in 19th-century schools was savage, and children who were poor at lessons were humiliated by being forced to wear a cap with the word “dunce” on it.
  • 19th-century clothes and inventions
    • In the 19th century, apart from cotton shirts, men’s clothes consisted of three parts: in the 18th century they wore knee-length breeches, but in the 19th century men wore trousers, along with waistcoats and coats.
    • In the early 19th century women wore light dresses; in the 1830s they had puffed sleeves; in the 1850s they wore frames of whalebone or steel wire called crinolines under their skirts; in the late 1860s women began to wear a kind of half crinoline — the front of the skirt was flat but it bulged outwards at the back, and this was called a bustle, which disappeared in the 1890s.
    • From the 1840s onwards it was fashionable for women to have very small waists, so they wore corsets.
    • About 1800 women started wearing underwear for the first time: they were called drawers, and originally women wore a pair of drawers — actually two garments, one for each leg, tied together at the top; in the late 19th century women’s drawers were called knickerbockers and then just knickers.
    • The Victorians usually wore hats: wealthy men wore top hats, middle-class men wore bowler hats and working men wore cloth caps.
    • Before the 19th century children were always dressed like little adults; in Victorian times the first clothes made especially for children appeared, such as sailor suits.
    • A number of inventions to do with clothing were made in the 19th century: the safety pin was invented in 1849; the electric iron was invented by Henry Seely in 1882 but did not become common until the 1930s; dry cleaning was invented in 1855; the zip fastener was invented in 1893; and in 1863 Butterick made the first paper dress pattern.
  • 19th-century documents and Queen Victoria
    • Act for the Union of Great Britain and Ireland (1800): since the grant of Ireland to Henry II in 1155/6 by Pope Adrian IV and Henry’s visit to Ireland in 1171/2, Ireland was a lordship attached to the English Crown, a jurisdiction separate from that of England; it remained so until 1541.
    • Throughout the Middle Ages Ireland had its own parliament, though its powers were limited; the Irish Parliament passed the Crown of Ireland Act 1542, proclaiming Henry VIII King of Ireland, which made Ireland a sister kingdom of England sharing the same monarch; both Ireland and England had come in personal union with Scotland with the Union of the Crowns in 1603.
    • After centuries of subordination to the English and later British Parliaments, the Parliament of Ireland gained a large measure of independence by the Constitution of 1782; many members jealously guarded its autonomy (notably Henry Grattan) and a motion for union was rejected in 1799.
    • Only Anglicans were permitted to become members of the Parliament of Ireland, though the great majority of the Irish population were Roman Catholic, with many Presbyterians in Ulster; in 1793 Roman Catholics regained the right to vote if they owned or rented property worth £2; the Catholic hierarchy was strongly in favour of union, hoping for rapid emancipation — the right to sit as MPs — which was however delayed until 1829.
    • The Irish Rebellion of 1798 brought the Irish question forcibly to the attention of the British Cabinet, and William Pitt the Younger decided that the best solution was a union; a union, Pitt argued, would both strengthen the connection between the two countries and provide Ireland with opportunities for economic development.
    • It would also, he thought (mistakenly), make it easier to grant concessions to the Roman Catholics, since they would be a minority in a United Kingdom; naturally, the union met with strong resistance in the Irish Parliament — whereas the first attempt had been defeated in the Irish House of Commons by 109 votes against to 104 for, the second vote in 1800 produced a result of 158 to 115.
    • The Act of Union with Ireland was signed by George III in August 1800 to become effective on 1 January 1801; henceforth the monarch was called the king (or queen) of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland.
    • The Acts ratified eight articles: Ireland was to be joined to Great Britain into a single kingdom, the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland; the Dublin parliament was abolished and Ireland was to be represented at Westminster by 100 MPs, 4 Lords Spiritual and 28 Lords Temporal (all Anglicans); the Anglican Church was to be recognised as the official Church of Ireland; there was to be free trade between Ireland and Britain; Ireland was to keep a separate Exchequer and be responsible for two-seventeenths of the general expense of the United Kingdom; Ireland kept its own Courts of Justice and civil service; no Catholics were to be allowed to hold public office; and there was to be no Catholic Emancipation.
    • The distinctively Catholic faith of the Irish was disparaged by the Act of Union; the new kingdom was from then onwards unambiguously called the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland; the flag, created as a consequence of the union, still remains the flag of the United Kingdom — called the Union Flag, it combined the flags of England (which included Wales) and Scotland with a “St Patrick’s Cross” to represent Ireland.
    • The Union was in being only for a few weeks when it received a body blow: George III flatly refused to consider Catholic emancipation and declared “I shall reckon any man my personal enemy who proposes any such measure”; Pitt did not see the Act of Union as a solution to the Irish problem, knowing that social and economic reforms were essential, as was Catholic Emancipation, so he resigned in protest on 3 February 1801 because he had intended to follow the Act of Union with reforms.
    • Over the next couple of decades resolutions and bills in favour of emancipation were debated at Westminster but failed to gain sufficient support; in the end emancipation was not granted, the opportunity to incorporate educated and propertied Irish Catholics into the élite was lost, and they formed their own alternative élite and called for repeal of the Union.
    • Ruling Ireland direct from Westminster solved nothing: the union was a political expedient over land, religion or politics, it had no social dimension at all, and Ireland’s economic problems were ignored — the Act did increase the sense of grievance in Ireland however.
    • The union remained until the recognition of the Irish Free State (excluding six of the counties of the northern province of Ulster) by the Anglo-Irish treaty concluded on 6 December 1921; the union officially ended on 15 January 1922 when it was ratified by the Provisional Government led by Michael Collins in Ireland; on 29 May 1953, by proclamation, Elizabeth II became known as queen of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland.
    • Key articles: Article first — the kingdoms of Great Britain and Ireland shall, upon 1 January 1801 and forever after, be united into one kingdom by the name of “the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland”; Article third — the United Kingdom shall be represented in one and the same parliament, styled “The Parliament of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland”.
    • Article fourth — four Lords Spiritual of Ireland by rotation of sessions and 28 Lords Temporal of Ireland elected for life by the peers of Ireland shall be the number to sit and vote on the part of Ireland in the House of Lords, and 100 commoners shall be the number to sit and vote on the part of Ireland in the House of Commons; Article fifth — the churches of England and Ireland as now by law established shall be united into one Protestant Episcopal Church, to be called “The United Church of England and Ireland”, and the continuance and preservation of the said united church shall be deemed and taken to be an essential and fundamental part of the union.
    • Article sixth — from 1 January 1801 all prohibitions and bounties on the export of articles, the growth, produce or manufacture of either country, to the other, shall cease and determine; Article seventh — for the space of twenty years after the union the contribution of Great Britain and Ireland respectively towards the expenditure of the United Kingdom shall be defrayed in the proportion of fifteen parts for Great Britain and two parts for Ireland.
    • People’s Charter (1838): Chartism was born amid the economic depression of 1837–38, when high unemployment and the effects of the Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 were felt in all parts of Britain.
    • The Poor Law Amendment Act 1834, known widely as the New Poor Law, was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom passed by the Whig government of Earl Grey that reformed the country’s poverty relief system (excepting Scotland, which reformed its poor law in 1845).
    • It was designed to reduce the cost of looking after the poor as it stopped money going to poor people except in exceptional circumstances; now if people wanted help they had to go into a workhouse to get it — the poor were given clothes and food in the workhouse in exchange for several hours of manual labour each day, families were split up inside the workhouse, people had to wear a type of uniform, follow strict rules and were on a bad diet of bread and watery soup; conditions were made so terrible that only those people who desperately needed help would go there.
    • Chartism was launched in 1838 by a series of enormous meetings in Birmingham, Glasgow and the north of England; there were different types of Chartists: Moral Force Chartism led by William Lovett wanted to achieve reform through peaceful protest and presented petitions to Parliament, while Physical Force Chartism led by Feargus O’Connor felt that only a violent campaign would achieve the vote for all men and gave speeches about how Chartists should be prepared to die for the cause.
    • In June 1839 the petition, signed by almost 1.3 million working people, was presented to the House of Commons; this document, written in 1838 mainly by William Lovett of the London Working Men’s Association, stated the ideological basis of the Chartist movement and detailed the six key points that the Chartists believed were necessary to reform the electoral system and thus alleviate the suffering of the working classes.
    • (1) A vote for all men over 21 apart from those convicted of a felony or declared insane, because only 18 per cent of the adult-male population of Britain could vote and before 1832 just 10 per cent could vote.
    • (2) A secret ballot, because voting at the time was done in public using a “show of hands” at the hustings, so landlords or employers could see how their tenants or employees were voting and could intimidate them; voting was not made secret until 1871.
    • (3) Electoral districts of equal size, because there were still great differences between constituencies, particularly in the industrial north where there were relatively few MPs compared to rural areas; the Chartists proposed the division of the United Kingdom into 300 electoral districts, each containing an equal number of inhabitants, with no more than one representative from each district.
    • (4) No property qualification to become an MP, because at that time potential members of Parliament needed to own property of a particular value, which prevented the vast majority of the population from standing for election.
    • (5) Payment for MPs, because MPs were not paid for the job they did, so only people with considerable personal wealth could afford to become MPs; the Charter proposed that MPs were paid an annual salary of £500.
    • (6) Annual elections for Parliament, to present the most effectual check to bribery and intimidation, since though a constituency might be bought once in seven years (even with the ballot), no purse could buy a constituency in each ensuing twelvemonth.
    • The aims may seem mild and sensible to us, but to the government of Victorian England they represented a potential for upheaval and overthrow of social institutions and entrenched authority; the violent turmoil of the French Revolution (1789–1799) was still fresh in the minds of many in positions of authority, and rather than being swayed by the sensibilities of the Chartist demands they reacted in fear at the possibility of violent overthrow of society and their own positions.
    • Though supported by future Prime Minister Benjamin Disraeli, the charter was rejected by the House of Commons by a vote of 235 to 46; this annoyed the more extreme Chartists led by Feargus O’Connor, militants called for rebellion, and there were riots in Newcastle, Birmingham and elsewhere where leading Chartists were arrested.
    • The only armed uprising was in Newport, Wales, in November 1839, and it went badly wrong: when they stormed a hotel to free their recently arrested colleagues, 20 Chartists were killed by waiting troops.
    • For a while the energy went out of the movement, though the National Charter Association was established in 1840 to co-ordinate work across the country; eventually the Chartists split into several factions and their influence declined, with the last great burst in 1848, when another convention was summoned and another petition was prepared — again Parliament did nothing; thereafter Chartism lingered another decade in the provinces, but its appeal as a national mass movement was ended.
    • Chartism failed for a number of reasons: most obviously it failed to gather support in Parliament, and equally important it failed to gather support from the middle classes, for whom the demands were too radical; the repeal of the Corn Laws helped improve the economic climate of Britain so there was less interest in radical reform, and the mid-19th century spawned a variety of social-reform groups with special aims, so the Chartist movement lost many of its members to these other groups.
    • Although the Chartist Movement failed to directly achieve its aims, the movement itself was not a failure at all, but a powerful force that resulted in an increased awareness of social issues and created a framework for future working-class organisations; with the onset of the relative prosperity of mid-Victorian Britain popular militancy lost its edge, but the Chartist spirit outlasted the organisation, and five of the six points — all except annual Parliaments — have since been secured.
    • Proclamation by the Queen in Council, to the princes, chiefs and people of India (1858): the proclamation confirms in their several offices, civil and military, all persons then employed in the service of the East India Company, subject to future pleasure and to such laws and regulations as might thereafter be enacted.
    • It announces to the native Princes of India that all treaties and engagements made with them by or under the authority of the East India Company are accepted and will be scrupulously maintained, and it looks for the like observance on their part.
    • It states that the Crown desires no extension of its present territorial possessions and will permit no aggression upon its dominions or rights with impunity, but will sanction no encroachment on those of others; it shall respect the rights, dignity and honour of native Princes as its own.
    • It is the further will that subjects, of whatever race or creed, be freely and impartially admitted to offices in the service, the duties of which they may be qualified by their education, ability and integrity duly to discharge.
    • It respects the feelings of attachment with which the natives of India regard the lands inherited from their ancestors and desires to protect them in all rights connected therewith, subject to the equitable demands of the State, and it wills that generally, in framing and administering the law, due regard be paid to the ancient rights, usages and customs of India.
    • Clemency will be extended to all offenders save and except those who have been or shall be convicted of having directly taken part in the murder of British subjects — with regard to such, the demands of justice forbid the exercise of mercy.
    • Queen Victoria (1819–1901) was the longest reigning British monarch and the figurehead of a vast empire; she oversaw huge changes in British society and gave her name to an age.
    • Victoria was born in London on 24 May 1819, the only child of Edward, Duke of Kent, and Victoria Maria Louisa of Saxe-Coburg; she succeeded her uncle William IV in 1837 at the age of 18, and her reign spanned the rest of the century.
    • On 10 February 1840, only three years after taking the throne, Victoria married her cousin Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg-Gotha; their relationship was one of great love and admiration, and together they had nine children — four sons and five daughters: Victoria, Bertie, Alice, Alfred, Helena, Louise, Arthur, Leopold and Beatrice — many of whom eventually married into the European monarchy.
    • On her accession Victoria adopted the Whig prime minister Lord Melbourne as her political mentor, but Prince Albert replaced Melbourne as the dominant male influence in her life; she was thoroughly devoted to him and completely submitted to his will, doing nothing without her husband’s approval.
    • Albert assisted in her royal duties, introduced a strict decorum in court and made a point of straitlaced behaviour; he also gave a more conservative tinge to Victoria’s politics; the German prince never really won the favour of the British public, and only after 17 years was he given official recognition with the title of “prince consort”.
    • During Albert’s lifetime Victoria was most active as a ruler; Britain was evolving into a constitutional monarchy in which the monarch had few powers and was expected to remain above party politics, although Victoria did sometimes express her views very forcefully in private.
    • Victoria never fully recovered from Albert’s death in 1861 and she remained in mourning for the rest of her life; this genuine but obsessive mourning kept her occupied and played an important role in the evolution of what would become the Victorian mentality; her subsequent withdrawal from public life made her unpopular, but during the late 1870s and 1880s she gradually returned to public view and, with increasingly pro-imperial sentiment, was restored to favour with the British public.
    • After the Indian Mutiny in 1857 the government of India was transferred from the East India Company to the Crown; in 1876 she was crowned Empress of India by Disraeli; her empire also included Canada, Australia, New Zealand and large parts of Africa; during this period Britain was largely uninvolved in European affairs, apart from the Crimean War from 1853 to 1856.
    • Victoria’s Golden Jubilee in 1887 and her Diamond Jubilee in 1897 were celebrated with great enthusiasm; having witnessed a revolution in British government, huge industrial expansion and the growth of a worldwide empire, Victoria died on 22 January 1901 at Osborne House on the Isle of Wight; the national pride connected with the name of Victoria — the term Victorian England, for example — stemmed from the Queen’s ethics and personal tastes, which generally reflected those of the middle class.

Seminar 6 — Britain in the 20th – Early 21st Century

Central concept: from the Edwardian era through two world wars, the welfare state and devolution to Brexit (1901–2023).

  • Timeline: early 20th century and the World Wars
    • 1901 Queen Victoria dies and her son becomes Edward VII; 1904 Britain signs the Entente Cordiale (friendly understanding) with France; 1906 the first dreadnought, a new and powerful battleship, is launched; 1908 Kenneth Grahame publishes The Wind in the Willows.
    • 1909 the first old age pensions are paid; 1910 Edward VII dies and George V becomes king; 1911 the National Insurance Act is passed (some workers are allowed unemployment benefit and sickness benefit is created) and the Parliament Act greatly reduces the power of the House of Lords.
    • 1914 in August the First World War begins; 1916 the Battle of the Somme is fought (tanks are used in battle for the first time), conscription is introduced, and the Battle of Jutland is fought; 1917 the Battle of Passchendaele is fought.
    • 1918 the First World War ends in November, women over 30 are allowed to vote, and the school leaving age is raised to 14.
    • 1922 the BBC is founded; 1926 workers hold a General Strike but they are defeated, and A. A. Milne publishes Winnie the Pooh; 1928 universal suffrage is introduced (everyone over the age of 21 is allowed to vote).
    • 1930 Frank Whittle invents the jet engine, and following the Wall Street Crash the depression bites and unemployment rises sharply; 1932 unemployment reaches 22.8%; 1933 unemployment starts to fall and Britain starts to recover.
    • 1936 unemployment is 13.9% in January and continues to fall slowly; George V dies, Edward VIII becomes king but soon abdicates, and George VI becomes king; television begins in Britain; the Jarrow March is held; the writer G. K. Chesterton dies.
    • 1939 the Second World War begins, all workers are given one week’s annual paid holiday, and about 10% of households own a car.
    • 1940 the British army is evacuated from Dunkirk; the Battle of Britain is fought; the Germans begin bombing British cities.
    • 1942 the Dieppe Raid is carried out and is a failure; the Battle of El Alamein is fought and the British army crushes the Germans and Italians; the Beveridge Report is published, proposing a new welfare state.
    • 1943 in May German forces in North Africa surrender and in July the Allies invade Sicily; 1944 the Allied invasion of France takes place, the Germans fire V1 flying bombs from June and V2 missiles from September, and the Butler Education Act is passed.
    • 1945 the Second World War ends, George Orwell’s Animal Farm is published, and Labour win the general election.
  • Timeline: post-war, affluence and the Thatcher years
    • 1947 the school leaving age is raised to 15; 1948 the National Health Service is founded; 1949 Orwell’s 1984 is published; 1950 Orwell dies.
    • 1951 the Festival of Britain is held and the Conservatives win the general election; 1952 George VI dies and Elizabeth becomes queen; 1953 the coronation of Elizabeth II takes place and TV becomes increasingly common — many people buy a TV to watch the coronation, and by the end of 1953 approximately 25% of households have one.
    • 1954 food rationing ends; 1955 the Conservatives win a second election; 1956 the Suez crisis occurs in Egypt — Britain sends troops but is soon forced to withdraw, and the event proves that Britain is no longer a great power; the first nuclear power station opens at Calder Hall.
    • 1959 the Conservatives win a third election victory; cars are becoming increasingly common and a survey shows 32% of households own one; 1960 a survey shows 44% of households own a washing machine.
    • 1962 the Beatles release their first single “Love Me Do”; 1964 the Labour Party wins the general election and Harold Wilson becomes prime minister; a survey shows 90% of households own a TV, which is now the main form of entertainment; the last executions in Britain are carried out.
    • 1965 capital punishment is abolished for an experimental period of 5 years; 1966 Labour wins a second election; 1967 colour TV begins; 1969 capital punishment is abolished permanently.
    • 1970 the Conservatives under Edward Heath win the general election and the minimum age for voting is lowered from 21 to 18; 1971 Britain switches to decimal currency; 1972 the school leaving age is raised to 16.
    • 1973 Britain joins the EEC (forerunner of the EU) and unemployment stands at 3%; 1975 the Sex Discrimination Act is passed and unemployment passes one million — over 5% for the first time since 1945.
    • 1976 Harold Wilson resigns, and in June and July a heat wave creates a very hot summer and water shortages; 1978 the first test-tube baby is born; 1979 the Conservatives win the general election and Margaret Thatcher becomes Britain’s first woman prime minister.
    • 1980 Britain enters recession, unemployment rises sharply and reaches 2 million; 1981 Prince Charles marries Diana; 1982 the Falklands War is fought against Argentina and the Mary Rose is raised from the sea bed.
    • 1983 the Conservatives win a second election with a large majority; 1984 the miners’ strike begins; 1985 the miners’ strike ends and the miners are defeated; 1986 unemployment reaches a peak, with the “official” figure at 14.1%; 1987 corporal punishment ends in state schools.
    • 1990 Margaret Thatcher falls from power and is replaced by John Major; Britain enters a recession and unemployment starts to rise rapidly; the Conservatives win a fourth election; 1993 Britain starts to recover and unemployment starts to fall.
    • 1994 the Channel Tunnel opens and the National Lottery begins; 1997 Labour wins the general election.
  • Timeline: the 21st century
    • 2004 the population of England reaches 50 million; 2005 hunting foxes with dogs is banned; 2007 Gordon Brown becomes prime minister and Jacqui Smith becomes the first female Home Secretary; 2009 a severe recession afflicts the nation.
    • 2010 recovery from recession begins; after a general election the Conservatives and Liberals form a coalition government, David Cameron becomes prime minister, and Gordon Brown is succeeded as Leader of the Labour Party by Ed Miliband.
    • 2011 the wedding of Prince William and Catherine Middleton; 2012 London hosts the Olympic Games for the third time; 2013 same-sex marriage is legalized in the United Kingdom.
    • 2014 the Scottish independence referendum takes place and Scotland decides to remain part of the United Kingdom; 2015 Elizabeth II becomes the longest-reigning monarch in British history.
    • 2016 the United Kingdom votes to leave the European Union; Theresa May becomes the second female prime minister; Great Britain ranks second on the 2016 Summer Olympics medal table with 27 gold medals, the best result since 1908.
    • 2019 the motion to approve the Brexit withdrawal agreement (the “meaningful vote”) is rejected 202–432, the largest defeat on a government motion in history; Theresa May formally tenders her resignation and is succeeded by Boris Johnson.
    • 2020 the United Kingdom leaves the European Union; a national lockdown takes place due to the coronavirus pandemic, freedom of movement is restricted and becomes enforceable in law, and non-essential shops and services close.
    • 2021 death of Prince Philip, Duke of Edinburgh, at the age of 99; 2022 Queen Elizabeth II’s Platinum Jubilee marking 70 years on the throne, Liz Truss becomes prime minister, Elizabeth II dies, and Liz Truss resigns after 45 days and is succeeded by Rishi Sunak, England’s first British-Asian prime minister.
    • 2023 coronation of Charles III and Camilla, and Humza Yousaf succeeds Nicola Sturgeon as Leader of the SNP.
  • Life in the 20th – early 21st century: poverty, housing and domestic technology
    • At the beginning of the 20th century surveys showed that 25% of the population were living in poverty; in 1900 some women made their underwear from bags that grocers kept rice or flour in, poor children often did not wear underwear, and some poor families made prams from orange boxes.
    • In the 1920s in the poorest areas children played barefoot because they couldn’t afford boots or shoes; a charity called the Boot Fund provided shoes for poor children and by the end of that decade children normally wore them.
    • Even so, in 1939 many children from cities were evacuated to the countryside to be safe from bombing, and many of them had never seen the countryside before; worse, some of them were used to sleeping in their parents’ bed or even under it, and some poor children were not used to sleeping in a bed at all.
    • From July 1941 clothes were also rationed and you had to save up coupons to buy your clothes; during the Second World War it was necessary to save material so skirts were shorter and people were encouraged to “make do and mend”; women did not wear tights at that time (they were not invented until 1958) but did wear stockings, which were scarce, so some women tanned their legs brown and painted a seam on; clothes were rationed until 1949.
    • After 1945 things improved when child benefit was introduced, and by 1950 absolute poverty had almost disappeared from Britain — absolute poverty can be defined as not having enough money to eat an adequate diet or afford enough clothes.
    • At the start of the 20th century working-class homes had two rooms downstairs: the front room was kept for best and children were not allowed to play there, while the back room was the kitchen and where the family spent most of their time; most families cooked on a coal-fired stove called a grange, which also heated the room.
    • This lifestyle changed in the early 20th century as gas cookers became common — they did not heat the room, so people began to spend most of their time in the front room or living room by the fire; rising living standards meant it was possible to furnish all rooms properly, not just one, and during the 20th century ordinary people’s furniture greatly improved in quality and design.
    • In the 1920s and 1930s a new style of furniture and architecture was introduced called Art Deco, which used geometric shapes instead of the flowing lines of the earlier Art Nouveau; the name came from an exhibition held in Paris in 1925 called the Exposition Internationale des Arts Decoratifs.
    • At the beginning of the 20th century only rich people could afford electric light and other people used gas; ordinary people did not have electric light until the 1920s and 1930s.
    • In the early 20th century vacuum cleaners and washing machines were available but only rich people could afford them; they became more common in the 1930s though still expensive, and by 1959 about two thirds of British homes had a vacuum cleaner; however fridges and washing machines did not become really common till the 1960s.
    • The first practical electric fire was made in 1912 but they did not become common until the 1930s; central heating became common in the 1960s and 1970s, double glazing in the 1980s; plastic or PVC was first used in the 1940s, and by the 1960s all kinds of household goods from drain pipes to combs were made of plastic.
    • In 1900 about 90% of the population rented their home, but home ownership became more common during the 20th century and by 1939 about 27% of the population owned their own house; meanwhile the first council houses were built before the First World War, with more in the 1920s and 1930s and some slum clearance, though council houses remained rare until after the Second World War.
    • After 1945 many more council houses were built and they became common; in the early 1950s many homes still did not have bathrooms and only had outside lavatories, but the situation greatly improved in the late 1950s and 1960s.
    • In the 1950s and 1960s large-scale slum clearance took place when whole swathes of old terraced houses were demolished and high-rise flats replaced some of them; however flats proved unpopular with many people, and some people who lived in the new flats felt isolated — the old terraced houses may have been grim but at least they often had a strong sense of community, which was usually not true of the flats that replaced them.
    • Furthermore in 1968 a gas explosion wrecked a block of flats at Ronan Point in London and public opinion turned against them; in the 1970s the emphasis turned to renovating old houses rather than replacing them, and then in 1979 the government adopted a policy of selling council houses.
  • Life in the 20th – early 21st century: food, clothes and leisure
    • The diet of ordinary people greatly improved during the 20th century: in 1900 some families sat down to tea of a plate of potatoes and malnutrition was common among poor children, and food was also expensive — in 1914 a working-class family spent about 60% of their income on food, while by 1937 they only spent about 35%.
    • Moreover sweets were a luxury in 1914 but became much more common in the 1920s and 1930s; food was rationed during the Second World War; in the late 20th century convenience foods became far more common, partly because fridges, freezers and later microwave ovens became common (microwave ovens first became common in the 1980s).
    • The British diet also became more varied: Chinese and Indian takeaways and restaurants became common, as in the 1980s did hamburger and pizza chains.
    • Several new foods were invented in the 20th century: the ice cream cone in 1903, hot dogs in 1908, choc-ices in the USA in 1921, sliced bread in the USA in 1928 (on sale in Britain in 1930), Spam in 1936, instant coffee in 1938, tea bags in 1953, fish fingers in 1955, the non-stick frying pan in 1954, and fruit-flavoured yoghurt in Britain in 1963.
    • Many new kinds of sweets were introduced, including Milky Way (1923), Crunchie (1929), Snickers (1930), Mars Bar (1933), Aero and Kit Kat (1935), Maltesers and Blue Riband (1936) and Smarties and Rolos (1937); later came Polo mints (1948), Bounty (1951), Yorkie and Lion Bar (1976) and Twix (1979); new biscuits included the bourbon (1910) and HobNobs (1986), and the ice lolly was invented in 1924.
    • At the end of the 20th century the first genetically modified foods were introduced, and today scientists are developing soya beans that can help prevent heart attacks, tomatoes that can help to prevent cancer, and rice that can resist drought and needs fewer nutrients.
    • The first supermarket in Britain opened in 1948, and in the 1950s and 1960s supermarkets replaced many small shops; credit cards became available in 1966, and at the end of the 20th century shopping on the internet became popular.
    • Clothes: at the beginning of the 20th century fashionable men wore trousers, waistcoat and coat, and top hats or homburgs; in 1900 women wore long dresses and it was not acceptable for women to show their legs; from 1910 women wore hobble skirts, so narrow that women could only “hobble” along while wearing them.
    • During the First World War women’s clothes became more practical; meanwhile in 1913 Mary Crosby invented the bra, using two handkerchiefs joined by ribbon, and in 1915 lipstick was sold in tubes for the first time.
    • In the early 1920s women still wore knickers that ended below the knee, but during the 1920s knickers became much shorter and by the late 1920s they ended well above the knee; during the 1940s and 1950s younger women wore briefs.
    • A revolution in women’s clothes occurred in 1925, when women began wearing knee-length skirts; in the mid and late 1920s it was fashionable for women to look boyish, but in the 1930s women’s dress became more conservative.
    • During the Second World War it was necessary to save material so skirts were shorter, and clothes were rationed until 1949; meanwhile the bikini was invented in 1946, and in 1947 Christian Dior introduced the New Look with long skirts and narrow waists giving an “hour glass” figure.
    • During the 1950s women’s clothes were full and feminine, but in 1965 Mary Quant invented the mini skirt and clothes became even more informal.
    • After the First World War men’s clothes became less informal and more casual: in the 1920s wide trousers called “Oxford bags” were fashionable, and men also often wore pullovers instead of waistcoats.
    • In the 19th century men’s underwear covered almost the whole body, stretching from the ankles to the neck and wrists, but in the 1920s men began to wear shorts that ended above the knee and sleeveless vests, and the first y-fronts went on sale in the mid-1930s.
    • In the second half of the 20th century fashions for both sexes became so varied and changed so rapidly that it would take too long to list them all; one of the biggest changes was the availability of artificial fibres: nylon was first made in 1935 by Wallace Carothers, polyester was invented in 1941 and became common in the 1950s, vinyl (a substitute for leather) was invented in 1924, and trainers were designed in 1949 by Adolf Dasler.
    • During the 20th century people had more and more leisure time: in 1900 the average working week was 54 hours, by the 1980s it was 39 hours; furthermore in 1900 most people had no paid holidays except bank holidays, but in 1939 a new law said that everyone must have one week’s annual paid holiday, by the 1950s two weeks were common, and by the 1980s most people had at least 4 weeks annual holiday.
    • In 1900 Frank Hornby invented a toy called Meccano; in 1907 Robert Baden-Powell formed the Boy Scouts; in 1910 the Girl Guides were formed; the first crossword was devised in 1913 by Arthur Wynne.
    • In the early 20th century films were often shown in theatres, but an increasing number of purpose-built cinemas appeared; the great age of cinema going was the 1930s when most people went at least once and sometimes twice a week; at first the films were silent but the first “talkie”, The Jazz Singer, was made in 1927; early films were also black and white, but in the 1930s the first colour films were made.
    • Radio broadcasting began in 1922 when the BBC was formed, and by 1933 half the households in Britain had a radio; television began in Britain in 1936 when the BBC began broadcasting, was suspended during the Second World War, and began again in 1946.
    • TV first became common in the 1950s: a lot of people bought a TV set to watch the coronation of Elizabeth II and a survey at the end of that year showed that about one quarter of households had one; by 1959 about two thirds of homes had a TV, and by 1964 the figure had reached 90%, with TV becoming the main form of entertainment at the expense of cinema.
    • At first there was only one TV channel, but between 1955 and 1957 the ITV companies began broadcasting, BBC2 began in 1964 and Channel 4 in 1982; in Britain BBC2 began broadcasting in colour in 1967 and BBC1 and ITV followed in 1969.
    • Video recorders became common in the early 1980s and many video hire shops opened at that time; at the end of the century videos were replaced by DVDs; portable TVs became common in the 1980s and satellite broadcasting began in 1989; satellite or cable TV became common in the 1990s; personal computers became common in the 1980s and the internet in the 1990s.
    • Furthermore in the late 20th century gardening became a very popular pastime, and so did DIY.
    • The new century has so far been a relatively successful one for the British film industry: many British films have found a wide international audience due to funding from BBC Films, Film 4 and the UK Film Council, and some independent production companies such as Working Title have secured financing and distribution deals with major American studios.
    • Working Title scored three major international successes, all starring Hugh Grant and Colin Firth, with the romantic comedies Bridget Jones’s Diary (2001, $254 million worldwide), the sequel Bridget Jones: The Edge of Reason ($228 million) and Richard Curtis’s directorial debut Love Actually (2003, $239 million); most successful of all was Phyllida Lloyd’s Mamma Mia! (2008), which grossed $601 million.
    • The new decade saw a major new film series in the US-backed but British-made Harry Potter films, beginning with Harry Potter and the Philosopher’s Stone in 2001; David Heyman’s company Heyday Films produced seven sequels, with the final two titles released in two parts in 2010 and 2011, all filmed at Leavesden Studios in England.
    • Popular music of the United Kingdom in the 2000s continued to expand and develop new sub-genres and fusions; in the 2000s new girl groups managed to enjoy sustained success, including the Sugababes and Girls Aloud.
    • The UK Office of National Statistics produced a new socio-economic classification in 2001, including Higher Professional and Managerial, Lower Managerial and Professional, Intermediate occupations, Small Employers and non-professional self-employed, Lower Supervisory and technical, Semi Routine Occupations, Routine Occupations, and Long-term unemployed groups.
  • Documents: Parliament Act, Fontainebleau, Statute of Westminster, Life Peerages, Human Rights
    • Parliament Act 1911: until the early years of the 20th century the House of Lords had the power to veto (stop) legislation.
    • In 1908 David Lloyd George, the Chancellor of the Exchequer in the Liberal government led by Herbert Asquith, introduced the Old Age Pensions Act that provided between 1s. and 5s. a week to people over seventy; to pay for these pensions Lloyd George had to raise government revenues by an additional £16 million a year.
    • In 1909 Lloyd George announced what became known as the People’s Budget, which included increases in taxation: people on lower incomes were to pay 9d. in the pound, those on annual incomes of over £3,000 had to pay 1s. 2d. in the pound, and he also introduced a new supertax of 6d. in the pound for those earning £5,000 a year; other measures included an increase in death duties on the estates of the rich and heavy taxes on profits gained from the ownership and sale of property.
    • The Conservatives, who had a large majority in the House of Lords, objected to this attempt to redistribute wealth and made it clear they intended to block these proposals; their veto held up the national finances and caused a struggle between the two houses.
    • Lloyd George reacted by touring the country making speeches in working-class areas on behalf of the budget and portraying the nobility as men who were using their privileged position to stop the poor from receiving their old age pensions; to solve the crisis two general elections were called in 1910, and the second gave authority to carry a Parliament Bill that would end such struggles.
    • When the House of Lords attempted to stop this bill’s passage, the Prime Minister Henry Asquith appealed to King George V for help; Asquith, who had just obtained a victory in the 1910 General Election, was in a strong position, and the king agreed that if necessary he would create 250 new Liberal peers to remove the Conservative majority in the Lords.
    • Faced with the prospect of a House of Lords with a permanent Liberal majority, the Conservatives agreed to let the 1911 Parliament Act become law; thus the Act was passed by the House of Lords by a 131–114 vote on 10 August 1911.
    • The Act removed from the House of Lords the power to veto a Bill, except one to extend the lifetime of a Parliament; any bill passed by the House of Commons in three separate sessions without being altered could be presented for royal assent without the consent of the Lords, providing that two years had elapsed since the bill had been introduced.
    • The Act also reduced the maximum lifespan of a Parliament from seven years to five; it put on the statute book the primacy of the House of Commons over financial legislation, so that financial measures could now be presented one month after they passed the House of Commons.
    • In subordinating the House of Lords to the House of Commons, the 1911 Act was regarded as another step in the gradual democratization of the British Constitution and had a profound effect on Parliament and politics in the 20th century.
    • Sections: Section 1 (Money Bills) — if a Money Bill, having been passed by the Commons and sent up to the Lords at least one month before the end of the session, is not passed by the Lords without amendment within one month, the Bill shall be presented to His Majesty and become an Act of Parliament on royal assent, notwithstanding that the Lords have not consented; Section 2 (other public bills) — if any Public Bill (other than a Money Bill or a Bill extending the maximum duration of Parliament beyond five years) is passed by the Commons in three successive sessions and rejected by the Lords in each of those sessions, that Bill shall, on its rejection for the second time by the Lords, be presented to His Majesty and become an Act on royal assent; Section 3 — any certificate of the Speaker given under this Act shall be conclusive for all purposes and shall not be questioned in any court of law; Section 6 — nothing in this Act shall diminish or qualify the existing rights and privileges of the House of Commons; Section 7 — five years shall be substituted for seven years as the maximum duration of Parliament under the Septennial Act 1715.
    • Fontainebleau Memorandum: the Armistice of 11 November 1918 was greeted with great joy, as the people of Europe wanted lasting peace and also to make Germany pay for the damage done and revenge.
    • In January 1919 delegates from 32 countries met in Paris to make peace after the First World War; the conference was dominated by David Lloyd George, Georges Clemenceau and Woodrow Wilson, the leaders of Britain, France and America, often known as the “Big Three”; negotiations were difficult, and by March 1919 it looked as though the conference was going to break up.
    • Wilson’s aims: to end war by creating a League of Nations based on his Fourteen Points, to ensure Germany was not destroyed, and not to blame Germany for the war (he hated the Guilt Clause).
    • Clemenceau’s aims: revenge and to punish Germany, to return Alsace-Lorraine to France, no League of Nations, an independent Rhineland, huge reparations, and to disband the German army so that Germany would never be strong enough to attack France again.
    • Lloyd George’s aims: a “just” peace that would be tough enough to please the electors who wanted to “make Germany pay” but would leave Germany strong enough to trade, land for Britain’s empire, and to safeguard Britain’s naval supremacy.
    • The Germans had expected that the peace treaty would be based on Wilson’s Fourteen Points, whose six key principles were: setting up a League of Nations, disarmament, self-determination for the people of Europe, freedom for colonies, freedom of the seas, and free trade; the Big Three instead expected to base the treaty on the terms of the armistice, which were much harsher: the German army disbanded and Germany to give up its navy, Allied troops to occupy the Rhineland, and reparation for damage done and war losses.
    • Lloyd George with his advisers retired to Fontainebleau to set out definitely what kind of peace treaty they would like to see, and on 25 March 1919 he issued the Fontainebleau Memorandum, titled “Some Considerations for the Peace Conference Before They Finally Draft Their Terms, March 25th, 1919”.
    • The memorandum called for a peace based on justice that would not provoke future wars; it also warned of the danger of provoking Germany into becoming Bolshevik and of the necessity of creating a League of Nations that would ensure international right and the abolition of competitive armaments.
    • The memorandum received a cool reception from Clemenceau, who argued that Germany was a menace to European peace and must be kept under control; he also pointed to the hypocrisy of the British in their insistence that the French make concessions while Britain kept all its German colonies — the seeds of post-war Anglo-French tensions were thus planted early.
    • The Germans were shown the proposed Treaty of Versailles with no negotiation; they published a rebuttal arguing that the treaty was unfair, but they were ignored, and on 28 June 1919 the delegates met at the Hall of Mirrors in the Palace of Versailles and forced two Germans (Foreign minister Hermann Müller and colonial minister Johannes Bell) to sign the treaty — without that national humiliation, Hitler would never have come to power and the Second World War would not have happened.
    • Excerpt points: Lloyd George was strongly adverse to transferring more Germans from German rule to the rule of some other nation than could possibly be helped; he could not conceive of any greater cause of future war than that the German people, one of the most vigorous and powerful races in the world, should be surrounded by a number of small states containing large masses of Germans clamoring for reunion with their native land.
    • The proposal of the Polish Commission to place 2,100,000 Germans under the control of a people of a different religion, which had never proved its capacity for stable self-government, must in his judgement lead sooner or later to a new war in the East of Europe.
    • The duration for the payments of reparations ought to disappear if possible with the generation which made the war; we shall never make lasting peace by attempting to restore the conditions of 1914, but there is a danger that we may throw the masses of the population throughout Europe into the arms of the extremists whose only idea for regenerating mankind is to destroy utterly the whole existing fabric of society.
    • The greatest danger in the present situation is that Germany may throw in her lot with Bolshevism and place her resources, her brains, her vast organizing power at the disposal of the revolutionary fanatics; this danger is no mere chimera, since the present government in Germany is weak, has no prestige and its authority is challenged.
    • If we are wise, we shall offer to Germany a peace which, while just, will be preferable for all sensible men to the alternative of Bolshevism; we cannot both cripple her and expect her to pay; we must offer terms which a responsible Government in Germany can expect to be able to carry out, because if we present terms which are unjust or excessively onerous, no responsible Government will sign them.
    • From every point of view, therefore, we ought to endeavor to draw up a peace settlement as if we were impartial arbiters, forgetful of the passions of the war; the settlement should have three ends in view: it must do justice to the Allies, it must be a settlement which a responsible German Government can sign in the belief that it can fulfil the obligations it incurs, and it must constitute an alternative to Bolshevism.
    • It is not enough, however, to draw up a just and far-sighted peace with Germany: if we are to offer Europe an alternative to Bolshevism we must make the League of Nations into something which will be both a safeguard to those nations prepared for fair dealing with their neighbours and a menace to those who would trespass on the rights of their neighbours.
    • An essential element is the constitution of the League of Nations as the effective guardian of international right and international liberty throughout the world; the first thing to do is that the leading members of the League should arrive at an understanding between themselves in regard to armaments, because it is idle to endeavor to impose a permanent limitation of armaments upon Germany unless we are prepared similarly to impose a limitation upon ourselves.
    • Finally, if the peace conference is really to secure peace it must deal with the Russian situation: Bolshevik Imperialism not merely menaces the states on Russia’s borders, it threatens the whole of Asia and is as near to America as it is to France.
    • Statute of Westminster (officially “An Act to give effect to certain resolutions passed by Imperial Conferences held in the years 1926 and 1930”) effected the equality of Britain and the dominions of Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Ireland and Newfoundland; it was passed on 11 December 1931, and the Empire’s time had practically come to an end.
    • The enactment of the Statute represents one of the most significant events in the history of the British Empire, recognizing significant advances in the evolution of the self-governing Dominions into fully sovereign states; the term “Dominion” was initially adopted in relation to Canada but was extended in 1907 to refer to all self-governing colonies of white settlement that had been evolving in the direction of greater autonomy since the middle of the nineteenth century.
    • The statute implemented decisions made at British imperial conferences in 1926 and 1930; the conference of 1926 in particular declared that the self-governing dominions were to be regarded as “autonomous communities within the British Empire, equal in status, in no way subordinate one to another, though united by a common allegiance to the Crown, and freely associated as members of the British Commonwealth of Nations”.
    • The statute recognized the sovereign right of each dominion to control its own domestic and foreign affairs, to establish its own diplomatic corps, and (except for Newfoundland) to be separately represented in the League of Nations.
    • Under the Statute, nations were granted the freedom to pass their own laws without the consent of the British Parliament, and Britain was no longer able to void or alter laws made in its Dominions; Dominions were also free to amend and repeal their own laws, including ones already in existence; in addition, laws passed by the British government would no longer extend to the Dominions unless the Dominion wished to adopt it.
    • All but one of the Dominions chose to adopt every resolution and thus sever all legal ties to Britain; Canada was not fully prepared for complete independence, and as government ministers were unable to agree upon a method which could be used to amend the Constitution, it was decided that Britain would temporarily retain the power to do so — this remained in effect until the Constitution Act was passed in 1982.
    • Since 1931 over a dozen new Commonwealth realms have been created, all of which now hold the same powers as the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia and New Zealand over matters of change to the monarchy; Ireland and South Africa are now republics and Newfoundland is part of Canada; the Irish Free State never formally adopted the Statute, though it still ended the ability of the British parliament to legislate for the Irish Free State without the latter’s request and consent.
    • The Statute was also a landmark document in terms of the Succession to the throne, enshrining the principle of the unity of the Crown and outlining, through its unenforceable preamble, the importance of cooperation and unanimity between the realms in order to successfully enact any changes to the succession; this principle was tested less than 5 years later, during the Abdication Crisis of King Edward VIII.
    • In 1936 a constitutional crisis in the British Empire was caused by King-Emperor Edward VIII’s proposal to marry Wallis Simpson, an American socialite who was divorced from her first husband and was pursuing a divorce of her second; the marriage was opposed by the governments of the United Kingdom and the autonomous Dominions of the British Commonwealth, and religious, legal, political and moral objections were raised.
    • As British monarch Edward was the nominal head of the Church of England, which did not allow divorced people to remarry if their ex-spouses were still alive, so it was widely believed that Edward could not marry Wallis Simpson and remain on the throne; Simpson was also perceived to be politically and socially unsuitable as a consort because of her two failed marriages, and it was widely assumed by the Establishment that she was driven by love of money or position rather than love for the King.
    • Despite the opposition, Edward declared that he loved Simpson and intended to marry her whether his governments approved or not; the widespread unwillingness to accept Simpson as the King’s consort, and Edward’s refusal to give her up, led to his abdication in December 1936.
    • He was succeeded by his brother Albert, who took the regnal name of George VI; Edward was given the title His Royal Highness the Duke of Windsor following his abdication, and he married Wallis Simpson the following year, remaining married until his death 35 years later.
    • Edward’s abdication required the consent of each Commonwealth state, which was duly given; however, the government of the Irish Free State, taking the opportunity presented by the crisis and in a major step towards its eventual transition to a republic, passed an amendment to its constitution on 11 December to remove references to the Crown, and the King’s abdication was recognised a day later in the External Relations Act, while legislation eventually passed in South Africa declared that the abdication took effect there on 10 December.
    • The statute left many difficult legal and constitutional questions unsettled — e.g. the functions of the Crown, and the possibility of one or more of the autonomous communities remaining neutral while others are at war — but mutual forbearance and constant consultation between the different units made the formula remarkably successful in operation.
    • Although the Statute’s continuing relevance is greatly reduced (and in many cases non-existent), by granting full independence to the self-governing dominions it is arguably the most important piece of legislation of the twentieth century.
    • Sections: 1 — “Dominion” means the Dominion of Canada, the Commonwealth of Australia, the Dominion of New Zealand, the Union of South Africa, the Irish Free State and Newfoundland; 4 — no Act of Parliament of the United Kingdom passed after the commencement of this Act shall extend to a Dominion as part of the law of that Dominion, unless it is expressly declared in that Act that that Dominion has requested and consented to the enactment thereof; 7(2) — the powers conferred by this Act upon the Parliament of Canada or upon the legislatures of the Provinces shall be restricted to the enactment of laws in relation to matters within the competence of the Parliament of Canada or of any of the legislatures of the Provinces respectively; 8 — nothing in this Act shall be deemed to confer any power to repeal or alter the Constitution or the Constitution Act of the Commonwealth of Australia or the Constitution Act of the Dominion of New Zealand otherwise than in accordance with the law existing before the commencement of this Act; 11 — the expression “Colony” shall not, in any Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom passed after the commencement of this Act, include a Dominion or any Province or State forming part of a Dominion.
    • Life Peerages Act 1958 (long title: “An Act to make provision for the creation of life peerages carrying the right to sit and vote in the House of Lords”) established the modern standards for the creation of life peers by the monarch of the United Kingdom.
    • The Act was made during the Conservative governments of 1957–1964, specifically under the earlier part of this period, when Harold Macmillan was Prime Minister; by the 1950s there was a feeling that the membership of the House of Lords ought to be tackled, and the Conservatives tried to introduce life peerages to further legitimise with modernity the House of Lords in 1958 and respond to a decline in its numbers and attendance; proposals for creating life peers, appointed by the Government for life rather than on a hereditary basis, had been around since the 1920s.
    • In November 1957 a Life Peerages Bill was introduced into the House of Lords; the clause relating to the creation of women peers caused the greatest agitation; an amendment to exclude women from the House was defeated at committee stage by 134 votes to 30; Viscount Astor, whose mother had been the first woman MP, was a great advocate for women peers, and Lord Chatfield said it was absurd to exclude representatives of half the nation.
    • The Labour Party opposed the Life Peerages Bill on Second Reading; the Bill passed its third reading in the Commons on 2 April 1958 by 292 votes to 241; although generally welcomed by peers, many felt that this reform did not go far enough, since hereditary peerages could still be created.
    • The Bill received Royal Assent on 30 April and the first 14 life Peers were announced on 24 July 1958; by virtue of the Act, four women took their seats in the House of Lords that year as life peers, while hereditary woman peers were not able to take their seats in the Lords until 1963.
    • Life peers are either barons or Baronesses (where female) and are members of the House of Lords for life, but their titles and membership in “the Lords” are not inherited by their children; the Act vastly increased the ability of the Prime Minister to change the composition of the House of Lords, gradually diminishing the numerical dominance of hereditary peers; a life peer is created by the sovereign by Letters Patent under the Great Seal on the advice of the Prime Minister.
    • The next Peerage Act (1963) received Royal Assent on 31 July 1963; it permitted peeresses in their own right and all Scottish hereditary peers to sit in the House of Lords, and allowed newly inherited hereditary peerages to be “disclaimed” — a peer who disclaims the peerage loses all titles, rights and privileges associated with the peerage, and if he is a married man, so does his wife.
    • The 1963 Act granted Peers of Scotland the same right to sit in the House of Lords as Peers of England, Great Britain or the United Kingdom, thereby ending the election of representative peers; an amendment that would have allowed Irish peers to sit in the House as well was defeated by 90 votes to 8.
    • The reform provided by the Life Peerages Act 1958 was a successful single-stage reform of the operation of the second Chamber, and history has proved that Conservative Ministers were right; in modern practice, only members of the Royal Family are granted new hereditary peerages.
    • Section 1(2): a peerage conferred under this section shall, during the life of the person on whom it is conferred, entitle him to rank as a baron under such style as may be appointed by the letters patent, and, subject to subsection (4), to receive writs of summons to attend the House of Lords and sit and vote therein accordingly, and shall expire on his death; 1(3) a life peerage may be conferred under this section on a woman; 1(4) nothing in this section shall enable any person to receive a writ of summons to attend the House of Lords, or to sit and vote in that House, at any time when disqualified therefor by law.
    • Human Rights Act 1998: the aftermath of the Second World War brought demands for an international forum to safeguard basic human rights; influenced by the Universal Declaration of Human Rights adopted by the UN General Assembly in 1948, the Council of Europe created the Convention for the Protection of Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms in 1950 (the European Convention on Human Rights).
    • As a founding member of the Council of Europe, the UK acceded to the European Convention on Human Rights in March 1951; the Convention took effect on 3 September 1953, but it was not until the 1960s that British citizens were able to bring claims in the European Court of Human Rights.
    • During the 1980s groups such as Charter 88 (which invoked the 300th anniversary of the Glorious Revolution in 1688 and the Bill of Rights 1689) accused the executive of misusing its power and argued that a British Bill of Rights was needed to secure human rights in the UK.
    • In 1997 the Labour government announced their intention to incorporate the convention into domestic law and published a white paper, Rights Brought Home: The Human Rights Bill, which would “mean that the British people will be able to argue for their rights in the British courts – without … inordinate delay and cost. It will also mean that the rights will be brought much more fully into the jurisprudence of the courts throughout the United Kingdom and will thus be far more subtly suited and powerfully woven into the law.”
    • Opponents expressed fears that the bill would shift authority from parliament to the judiciary; William Hague, leader of the Conservatives, stated that it would replace “the rule of law with the rule of lawyers”.
    • The bill was introduced by Lord Irvine to the House of Lords on 3 November 1997, and the second reading in the House of Commons took place on 16 February 1998; the Human Rights Act received Royal Assent on 9 November 1998 and mostly came into force on 2 October 2000.
    • The Act incorporates into British law key elements of the European Convention on Human Rights, including Article 2 (right to life), Article 3 (prohibition of torture), Article 4 (prohibition of slavery and forced labour), Article 5 (right to liberty and security), Article 6 (right to a fair trial), Article 7 (no punishment without law), Article 8 (right to respect for private and family life), Article 9 (freedom of thought, conscience and religion), Article 10 (freedom of expression), Article 11 (freedom of assembly and association), Article 12 (right to marry), Article 14 (prohibition of discrimination), Protocol No. 1 (protection of property, right to education, right to free elections) and Protocol No. 6 (abolition of the death penalty; death penalty in time of war).
    • The Act places a duty on all courts and tribunals in the United Kingdom to interpret legislation so far as possible in a way compatible with the rights laid down in the European Convention on Human Rights; it applies to all public bodies within the United Kingdom, including central government, local authorities and bodies exercising public functions, but it does not include Parliament when it acts in its legislative capacities.
    • The Act may be used by every person resident in England or Wales regardless of whether or not they are a British citizen or a foreign national, a child or an adult, a prisoner or a member of the public; it can even be used by companies or organisations.
    • Since the coalition government came into power on 11 May 2010 there has been an increasingly vociferous debate about the continuation of the HRA in its present form; proposals have been put forward to replace it with a “modern British bill of rights” and even more radical action has been suggested vis-à-vis this country’s relationship with the European Court of Human Rights.
    • Excerpt: 1 — everyone’s right to life shall be protected by law, and no one shall be deprived of his life intentionally save in the execution of a sentence of a court following his conviction of a crime for which this penalty is provided by law; 3 — no one shall be subjected to torture or to inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment; 4 — no one shall be held in slavery or servitude, and no one shall be required to perform forced or compulsory labour; 5 — everyone has the right to liberty and security of person; 6 — in the determination of his civil rights and obligations or of any criminal charge against him, everyone is entitled to a fair and public hearing within a reasonable time by an independent and impartial tribunal established by law, and everyone charged with a criminal offence shall be presumed innocent until proved guilty according to law; 8 — everyone has the right to respect for his private and family life, his home and his correspondence; 9 — everyone has the right to freedom of thought, conscience and religion; 10 — everyone has the right to freedom of expression, including freedom to hold opinions and to receive and impart information and ideas without interference by public authority and regardless of frontiers; 11 — everyone has the right to freedom of peaceful assembly and to freedom of association with others, including the right to form and to join trade unions for the protection of his interests; 14 — the enjoyment of the rights and freedoms set forth in this Convention shall be secured without discrimination on any ground such as sex, race, colour, language, religion, political or other opinion, national or social origin, association with a national minority, property, birth or other status; Article 1 of the Thirteenth Protocol — the death penalty shall be abolished, and no one shall be condemned to such penalty or executed.
  • Historic figures: Lloyd George, Churchill, Thatcher, Elizabeth II
    • David Lloyd George (1863–1945) was one of the great reforming British chancellors of the 20th century and prime minister from 1916 to 1922; born in Manchester on 17 January 1863, the son of a schoolmaster, he lost his father young and his mother took him to Wales to be raised, and he became a lifelong Welsh nationalist.
    • He qualified as a solicitor and in 1888 married Margaret Owen, the daughter of a prosperous farmer; he remained an active member of the Disciples of Christ Chapel, and it was during his church work that he gained his early training as an orator — he developed a reputation as a fiery preacher and was often asked to speak at Temperance Society meetings in Wales.
    • In 1890 he was elected Liberal member of parliament for Caernarvon, a seat he held until 1945; he quickly became known for his radicalism and earned notoriety for his opposition to the Boer War.
    • In 1905 the prime minister Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman appointed Lloyd George as president of the Board of Trade, and in 1908 he was named chancellor of the exchequer in the government of H. H. Asquith.
    • Lloyd George’s 1909 budget has been called the “People’s Budget” since it provided for social insurance that was to be partly financed by land and income taxes; the budget was rejected by the House of Lords, which led directly to the Parliament Act of 1911 by which the Lords lost their power of veto.
    • Lloyd George’s next reform was the 1911 National Insurance Act, which gave the British working classes the first contributory system of insurance against illness and unemployment; all wage-earners between sixteen and seventy had to join the health scheme.
    • His reforms were strongly criticised and some Conservatives accused him of being a socialist; there is no doubt that he had been heavily influenced by Fabian Society pamphlets on social reform written by Beatrice Webb, Sidney Webb and George Bernard Shaw in the early 1900s, although he had also been influenced by non-socialist writers such as Seebohm Rowntree and Charles Booth.
    • Although most Labour Party members of the House of Commons had welcomed Lloyd George’s reforms, politicians such as James Keir Hardie, Fred Jowett and George Lansbury argued that the level of benefits were far too low; they also complained that the pensions should be universal and disliked what was later to be called the Means Test aspect of these reforms.
    • Lloyd George remained chancellor of the exchequer through the early years of the First World War; in 1915 he was appointed minister of munitions in Asquith’s wartime coalition government, in July 1916 he became secretary of state for war, and in December 1916, with the support of the Conservative and Labour leaders, he replaced Asquith as prime minister.
    • His achievements in the last two years of the war included persuading the Royal Navy to introduce the convoy system and the unification of the Allied military command under the French general Ferdinand Foch.
    • At the successful conclusion of the war Lloyd George was Britain’s chief delegate to the Paris Peace Conference that drafted the Versailles Treaty, where he clashed with Georges Clemenceau about how the defeated powers should be treated — Lloyd George told Clemenceau that his proposals were too harsh and would “plunge Germany and the greater part of Europe into Bolshevism”, while Clemenceau replied that Lloyd George’s alternative proposals would lead to Bolshevism in France.
    • At the end of the negotiations Clemenceau managed to restore Alsace-Lorraine to France, but some of his other demands were resisted by the other delegates; Clemenceau, like most people in France, thought that Germany had been treated too leniently at Versailles.
    • Lloyd George remained prime minister, although now dependent on Conservative support; in 1921 he secured the settlement that established the Irish Free State; in the summer of 1922 he was involved in a scandal involving the selling of knighthoods and peerages, and in October the Conservatives withdrew from the coalition over their opposition to Britain’s foreign policy in Turkey; Lloyd George resigned as prime minister, remained in parliament, but was marginalised politically.
    • In September 1936 Lloyd George visited Adolf Hitler in an attempt to persuade him not to stop taking military action in Europe; after his arrival back in Britain he wrote in the Daily Express: “I have now seen the famous German leader and also something of the great change he has effected. Whatever one may think of his methods – and they are certainly not those of a Parliamentary country – there can be no doubt that he has achieved a marvellous transformation in the spirit of the people, in their attitude towards each other, and in their social and economic outlook. One man has accomplished this miracle. He is a born leader of men. A magnetic dynamic personality with a single-minded purpose, a resolute will, and a dauntless heart.”
    • Although Lloyd George agreed that Germany had been badly treated after the First World War, he was opposed to the British government’s policy of appeasement; in 1944 he was made Earl Lloyd George of Dwyfor, and he died on 26 March 1945 at Ty Newydd, Llanystumdwy, Wales.
    • Winston Churchill (1874–1965) was a politician and wartime prime minister who led Britain to victory in the Second World War; at the beginning of the war his reputation was that of a gifted politician who had twice changed parties, an impulsive man prone to impractical enthusiasms, and a Conservative backbencher who opposed the foreign policy of his leader Neville Chamberlain — six years later he towered above all contemporaries as a statesman of international renown.
    • Winston Leonard Spencer Churchill was born on 30 November 1874 at Blenheim Palace in Oxfordshire; his father was the prominent Tory politician Lord Randolph Churchill; he attended the Royal Military College, Sandhurst, before embarking on an army career, saw action on the North West Frontier of India and in the Sudan, and while working as a journalist during the Boer War was captured and made a prisoner-of-war before escaping.
    • In 1900 Churchill became Conservative member of parliament for Oldham, but he became disaffected with his party and in 1904 joined the Liberal Party; when the Liberals won the 1905 election he was appointed undersecretary at the Colonial Office; in 1908 he entered the Cabinet as president of the Board of Trade, becoming home secretary in 1910, and the following year first lord of the Admiralty.
    • He held this post in the first months of the First World War but after the disastrous Dardanelles expedition, for which he was blamed, he resigned; he joined the army, serving for a time on the Western Front, and in 1917 was back in government as minister of munitions; from 1919 to 1921 he was secretary of state for war and air, and from 1924 to 1929 chancellor of the exchequer.
    • The next decade were his “wilderness years”, in which his opposition to Indian self-rule and his support for Edward VIII during the Abdication Crisis made him unpopular, while his warnings about the rise of Nazi Germany and the need for British rearmament were ignored; when war broke out in 1939 Churchill became first lord of the Admiralty.
    • In May 1940 Neville Chamberlain resigned as prime minister and Churchill took his place; his refusal to surrender to Nazi Germany inspired the country, and “Winnie” changed his country’s military approach from defensiveness to aggressive attack, and so altered the course of history.
    • Churchill persuaded cabinet and parliament that Britain and its empire could survive; his inspiring speeches encouraged the British people to be courageous and hopeful, and he invited the rest of the world — especially the United States, whose support he hoped to secure — to back them up; he forbade defeatist talk and refused to be put on the defensive.
    • Even in those fraught days he ordered planning to begin for attacking German power by means of heavy bombing, commando raids and Special Operations Executive (SOE)-aided resistance by Germany’s victims; his public demeanour was unfailingly brave and heartening, the Conservative Party came round to him, the British people (except for the communists) were solidly behind him, and by the end of October the worst of the dangers of that year were past.
    • Besides being a popular leader, Churchill was also an emphatically democratic one: Parliament continued to sit throughout the war, and the war’s progress was publicly debated; Churchill assumed full responsibility and during the dark months of 1941–1942, when he often had to report disasters, he had to bow a bit to his critics — the normal peacetime freedoms of the citizen were of course restricted but rarely beyond the limits of reason.
    • Churchill lost power in the 1945 post-war election but remained leader of the opposition, voicing apprehensions about the Cold War (he popularised the term “Iron Curtain”) and encouraging European and trans-Atlantic unity; in 1951 he became prime minister again, resigned in 1955, but remained an MP until shortly before his death.
    • As well as his many political achievements, he left a legacy of an impressive number of publications and in 1953 won the Nobel Prize for Literature; Churchill died on 24 January 1965 and was given a state funeral.
    • Margaret Thatcher (1925–2013) was Britain’s first female prime minister and served three consecutive terms in office; when she became PM many people in Britain wondered whether a woman would be strong enough to do this very difficult job, and she showed them the answer was “yes”.
    • Margaret Hilda Roberts was born on 13 October 1925 in Grantham, Lincolnshire; her parents Alf and Beatrice Roberts ran a grocery and she lived over the shop, sometimes helping behind the counter; she had an older sister called Muriel, but no brothers or other close family.
    • During her childhood lots of people had no jobs and had very little money; the Roberts family was luckier than most and did baking for people who were unwell or didn’t have enough to eat, which people from their church took round.
    • She went to Oxford University and then became a research chemist, retraining to become a barrister in 1954; in 1951 she married a wealthy businessman, Denis Thatcher, who was older than her and had fought in the war — they were a very close couple and he gave her tremendous help and support in everything she did.
    • They had twins in 1953, Mark and Carol; in those days fathers weren’t usually present at the birth of their children — Denis was at a cricket match when they were born and when he first saw the babies said they looked like rabbits without fur on (he was always cracking jokes).
    • Thatcher became Conservative member of parliament for Finchley in north London in 1959, serving as its MP until 1992; her first parliamentary post was junior minister for pensions in Harold Macmillan’s government, and from 1964 to 1970, when Labour were in power, she served in a number of positions in Edward Heath’s shadow cabinet.
    • Heath became prime minister in 1970 and Thatcher was appointed secretary for education; she had a bad time as Education Minister, especially in her first year, and was attacked by people who were annoyed that she had abolished free school milk for older children (she got the nickname “Milk Snatcher”); there were lots of other arguments too, and she was unhappy for a time, but in the end she was made stronger by the experience and many of her critics came to realise how tough a person she was.
    • After the Conservatives were defeated in 1974, Thatcher challenged Heath for the leadership of the party and, to the surprise of many, won; in the 1979 general election the Conservatives came to power and Thatcher became prime minister.
    • An advocate of privatisation of state-owned industries and utilities, reform of the trade unions, the lowering of taxes and reduced social expenditure across the board, Thatcher’s policies succeeded in reducing inflation, but unemployment dramatically increased.
    • Victory in the Falklands War in 1982 and a divided opposition helped Thatcher win a landslide victory in the 1983 general election; in October 1984, when the strike was still going on, the Irish Republican Army attempted to murder Thatcher and many of her colleagues by bombing their hotel in Brighton during the Conservative Party annual conference — although she survived unhurt, some of her closest friends were among the injured and dead, and the room next to her bedroom was severely damaged, so the attackers came close to killing her.
    • In foreign affairs Thatcher cultivated a close political and personal relationship with US president Ronald Reagan, based on a common mistrust of communism combined with free-market economic ideology; Thatcher was nicknamed the “Iron Lady” by the Soviets, and she warmly welcomed the rise of reformist Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev.
    • In the 1987 general election Thatcher won an unprecedented third term in office, but controversial policies, including the poll tax and her opposition to any closer integration with Europe, produced divisions within the Conservative Party which led to a leadership challenge; in November 1990 she agreed to resign and was succeeded as party leader and prime minister by John Major.
    • After 1990 Lady Thatcher remained a powerful political figure; in 1992 she left the House of Commons for the House of Lords as Baroness Thatcher of Kesteven; she wrote two best-selling volumes of memoirs — The Downing Street Years (1993) and The Path to Power (1995) — and for 12 years toured the world lecturing.
    • In March 2002 she announced she was giving up public speaking, on doctor’s advice; Denis Thatcher, her husband for more than fifty years, died in June 2003, and she was very sad to lose him; the following year Thatcher had to say goodbye to an old friend and ally, Ronald Reagan; in 2011 the former prime minister was the subject of an award-winning (and controversial) biographical film, The Iron Lady, which depicted her political rise and fall.
    • People still argue a lot about Margaret Thatcher: critics claim that many of her policies were too harsh and hurt people, while defenders say she made the British economy work much better and that made most people better off; but critics and supporters agree in thinking that Margaret Thatcher’s time as PM was very important in British history — she died on 8 April 2013, at age 87.
    • Elizabeth II (1926–2022): Elizabeth Alexandra Mary was born on 21 April 1926 in London, the first child of Albert, Duke of York, and his wife, formerly Lady Elizabeth Bowes-Lyon; she initially had little prospect of succeeding to the throne until her uncle Edward VIII abdicated in December 1936, after which her father became George VI and she became heir.
    • Elizabeth and her younger sister Margaret were educated at home; on the outbreak of war in 1939 they were evacuated to Windsor Castle, and in 1945 Elizabeth joined the war effort, training as a driver in the Women’s Auxiliary Territorial Service (WATS).
    • In November 1947 she married a distant cousin, Philip Mountbatten (formerly Prince Philip of Greece and Denmark), who was created duke of Edinburgh; the couple have four children; the marriage was not without controversy, since Philip had no financial standing, was foreign-born (though a British subject), and had sisters who had married German noblemen with Nazi links, and Elizabeth’s mother was reported in later biographies to have opposed the union initially.
    • Elizabeth witnessed over her life the ongoing transformation of the British Empire into the Commonwealth of Nations; by the time of her accession in 1952 her role as nominal head of multiple independent states was already established.
    • George VI died on 6 February 1952 while Elizabeth and Philip were in Kenya; she returned home immediately and was crowned at Westminster Abbey in June 1953; spanning 1953–54, the Queen and her husband embarked on a six-month around-the-world tour, and she became the first reigning monarch of Australia and New Zealand to visit those nations.
    • In 1991, in the wake of victory in the Gulf War, she became the first British monarch to address a joint session of the United States Congress; the following year she attempted to save the failing marriage of her eldest son Charles by counselling him and his wife Diana, Princess of Wales, to patch up their differences.
    • The Queen and the Duke of Edinburgh celebrated their 60th wedding anniversary in 2007; their marriage is the longest of any British monarch; in 2002 Elizabeth celebrated her golden jubilee (50 years on the throne) and in 2006 her 80th birthday, and she celebrated her Diamond Jubilee in 2012, marking 60 years as Queen.
    • Elizabeth became the longest-lived British head of state, the longest-reigning monarch in the history of any of her realms, and the longest-reigning queen regnant in world history, surpassing Queen Victoria, who celebrated her Diamond Jubilee in 1897.
    • Elizabeth was the patron of over 600 charities and other organisations; her main leisure interests included equestrianism and dogs, especially her Pembroke Welsh Corgis; her clothes consisted mostly of solid-colour overcoats and decorative hats, which allowed her to be seen easily in a crowd.
    • In the 1950s, as a young woman at the start of her reign, Elizabeth was depicted as a glamorous “fairytale Queen”; after the trauma of the war it was a time of hope, a period of progress and achievement heralding a “new Elizabethan age”; despite the controversies and scandals surrounding her children and other members of the royal family, she remained a respected head of state.
    • In June 2022 Britain celebrated Elizabeth’s 70 years on the throne with the “Platinum Jubilee”; on 8 September 2022 Elizabeth’s death, at age 96, shocked Britain and the world; she was known to favour simplicity in court life and to take a serious and informed interest in government business, aside from the traditional and ceremonial duties, and her financial and property holdings made her one of the world’s richest women.

Seminar 8 — General Outline of the UK

Central concept: geography, territories, national parks, population, stereotypes and the four major cities of the UK.

  • Geography: location, relief, climate and resources
    • The UK consists of a group of islands off the north-west coast of Europe and is made up of four nations: England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland; England, Wales and Scotland also make up Great Britain; the UK lies to the north-west of Europe, borders France and Belgium by sea, stretches for about 900 km from north to south and 500–600 km from east to west, and no part of the country is more than 120 km away from the sea.
    • Geographically the UK is mostly a plain; England, unlike the largely mountainous countries of Wales and Scotland, is mainly lowland except for six major hilly regions: the Pennines (called the “backbone of England”, dividing the north-west of England from the north-east), the scenic Lake District in the north-west, the Yorkshire Dales running to the east coast of Yorkshire, the moorlands of Cornwall and Devon in the south-west, and the border areas with Scotland and Wales respectively.
    • Wales is almost entirely mountainous with a short strip of coast valleys in the south and west; Scotland is usually divided into three parts — the Highlands in the north, the Lowlands in the centre and the Uplands in the south; Northern Ireland consists of plain intermittent with uplands; the highest mountains are Ben Nevis in Scotland and Snowdon in Wales.
    • The largest lakes are Lough Neagh, Loch Erne, Loch Lomond and Loch Ness, and the largest rivers are the Severn, the Thames and the Trent.
    • The climate is mild and damp and is influenced by the warm current of the Gulf Stream; the most rainy months are April and October, the Lake District is England’s wettest region, and the average temperature in the south is between +5 and +16 °C.
    • The UK is bordered by four seas: to the south by the English Channel, which separates it from continental Europe; to the east by the North Sea; to the west by the Irish Sea and the Atlantic Ocean.
    • Resources: historically much of the United Kingdom was forested, but since prehistoric times man has deforested much of it; agriculture is intensive, highly mechanised and efficient by European standards, producing about 60% of food needs with only 1% of the labour force and contributing around 2% of GDP, with around two thirds of production devoted to livestock and one third to arable crops.
    • In 1993 land use was estimated as arable land 25%, permanent crops 0%, permanent pastures 46%, forests and woodland 10%, other 19%, and irrigated 1,080 km².
    • The UK has a variety of natural resources, including geological resources (coal, petroleum, natural gas, limestone, chalk, gypsum, silica, rock salt, china clay, iron ore, tin, silver, gold, lead) and agricultural resources (arable land, wheat, barley, sheep); it has large coal, natural gas and oil reserves, and primary energy production accounts for 10% of GDP, one of the highest shares of any industrial nation; due to its island location the country has great potential for generating electricity from wave power and tidal power, although these have not yet been exploited on a commercial basis.
  • British Overseas Territories and Crown Dependencies
    • A British Overseas Territory is one of fourteen territories which the United Kingdom considers to be under its sovereignty but not as part of the United Kingdom itself; before 1981 the territories were known as colonies or Crown colonies.
    • The head of state in the Overseas Territories is the British monarch, currently King Charles III, whose role in the territories is in his role as King of the United Kingdom and not in right of each territory; the King appoints a representative in each territory to exercise executive power.
    • All the Overseas Territories have their own system of government and localised laws, and the structure of government appears closely correlated to the size and political development of the territory; each has its own legal system independent of the United Kingdom, generally based on English common law with some distinctions for local circumstances, and each has its own Attorney General and court system.
    • For the smaller territories the UK may appoint a UK-based lawyer or judge to work on legal cases, which is particularly important for cases involving serious crimes and where it is impossible to find a jury who will not know the defendant in a small population island.
    • The fourteen BOTs, in alphabetical order, are Anguilla, Bermuda, the British Antarctic Territory, the British Indian Ocean Territory (BIOT), the British Virgin Islands (BVI), the Cayman Islands, the Sovereign Base Areas of Akrotiri and Dhekelia on Cyprus, the Falkland Islands, Gibraltar, Montserrat, the Pitcairn Islands, Saint Helena and its dependencies (Ascension Island and Tristan da Cunha), South Georgia and the South Sandwich Islands, and the Turks and Caicos Islands.
    • The overall population of the BOTs totals approximately 200,000, ranging from 67,000 in Bermuda to about 50 on the Pitcairn Islands; five BOTs — Anguilla, the BVI, the Cayman Islands, Montserrat and the Turks and Caicos Islands — are located in the Caribbean Sea, while Bermuda is situated in the North Atlantic off the US coast.
    • The Falklands, South Georgia and the South Sandwich Islands, Saint Helena, Tristan da Cunha and Ascension Island are all located in the South Atlantic; Tristan da Cunha is the most remote inhabited island in the world, about 1,700 miles west of Cape Town; the Pitcairn Islands are a group of four volcanic islands in the South Pacific 1,550 miles south-east of Tahiti, of which only Pitcairn Island is inhabited; the BIOT comprises the Chagos Archipelago and includes the atoll of Diego Garcia, lying between Africa and Indonesia.
    • The BOTs are constitutionally not part of the UK and each has a separate constitution; all have governors appointed by King Charles III except the uninhabited ones; each BOT is constitutionally unique, and the degree of self-government depends on the BOT’s constitutional relationship with the UK — larger, more developed BOTs are largely autonomous in regard to their internal affairs, as is the case with Bermuda, Gibraltar, the Falklands and others; the common thread among them is recognition of UK sovereignty, acknowledgment of the King as the Head of State, and British citizenship.
    • Tourism and financial services account for most of the revenue generated in the BOTs; shipping and the sale of fishing licenses are also revenue sources for many BOTs, and the BOTs also generate revenue from the sale of postage stamps.
    • A British Crown Dependency is a special type of state: the head of state is the British monarch, who is represented by a Lieutenant-Governor, and each dependency has a parliament, government and prime minister to make all laws except laws about defence and foreign affairs, which are looked after by the British Government — but the British government in London has no power in the dependencies unless the government of the dependency agrees.
    • The Crown Dependencies are the Isle of Man, the Bailiwick of Jersey, and the Bailiwick of Guernsey and its dependencies.
    • The Isle of Man is situated in the Irish Sea between the United Kingdom and Ireland; it is 22 km wide and 52 km long with a total area of 572 km², and apart from the island itself it also includes some nearby islands, the most important being the Calf of Man, St Patrick’s Isle and St Michael’s Isle.
    • The island’s terrain is varied, with mountains in the north and south and a valley more or less in the centre, while the northern part is very flat; Snaefell is the Isle of Man’s highest mountain at 621 metres above sea level, and it is said that you can see Scotland, England, Ireland and Wales from the top.
    • Jersey is an island off the north-west coast of France; it is called a bailiwick because the person who is the head of law for the island is called the Bailiff, and the Bailiwick of Jersey also includes the islands of the Minquiers and Ecréhous, where nobody lives; over ninety-seven thousand people live on the main island, thirty per cent of them in its only town, Saint Helier; the Bailiwick of Jersey is not part of the United Kingdom or of the European Union and has its own parliament called the States of Jersey which passes laws for the island.
    • Guernsey is an island in the English Channel off the coast of Normandy and is the main administration island of the Bailiwick of Guernsey; Charles III is the head of state, but it has a government of its own with its own laws and special status when it comes to taxes, and 65,573 people live there.
    • Guernsey has its own dialect of the Norman language called Guernésiais, which is spoken by only 2% of the population but understood by around 14% — meaning only about 1,300 people speak it, most of them elderly, and only 1 in every 1,000 young people can speak it.
  • National Parks of the UK
    • The National Parks in the UK protect some of the most spectacular and valued landscapes in England, Scotland and Wales, and they also protect farms, villages and cultural heritage; the 15 areas designated as national parks are not large virgin wilderness areas owned by public bodies but are in fact populated places with much of the land in private ownership, where people who farm the land or settle in the towns and villages take part in preserving Britain’s wild and worked landscapes.
    • They include mountains, moors, woodlands, marshland, pasture, lakes and coasts, and most UK national parks have numerous nature reserves that shelter rare species such as native red squirrels, wild ponies, orchids and ospreys; the oldest park, the Peak District, was founded in 1951, and one of the newest is the South Downs, established in April 2009.
    • Cairngorms: covering 1,467 square miles of central Scotland, it is the UK’s largest national park and also the northernmost national park in Great Britain; about 17,000 people live and work in the park, an area characterized by high arctic landscapes, wild and windswept mountains and primeval forests; the park has five of Scotland’s six highest mountains and boasts the cleanest rivers, lochs and marshes in Scotland, and the pine trees in its original Caledonian forests include a rare variety found only in Scotland and Norway.
    • Loch Lomond & the Trossachs: covers 720 square miles in the Scottish heartland, within an hour’s drive for more than 70% of Scotland’s population, and takes in a wide variety of landscapes — wild mountain glens, towering giants, rolling hill country, rivers, woodlands and lochs.
    • Northumberland: stretching from Hadrian’s Wall to the Cheviot Hills along the traditional Scottish border, it is one of England’s emptiest quarters, with only 2,200 people living within its 405 square miles midway between Carlisle and Newcastle-upon-Tyne; archaeological excavations show evidence of human occupation here for 10,000 years, and the mysterious hillforts that top many hills in the northern part were probably built about 700 BC; before the Norman Conquest this area was the centre of the Anglo-Saxon Kingdom of Northumbria.
    • Lake District: in England’s north-west, it is a vast national park carved out by glaciers about 15,000 years ago, with four mountains over 3,000 feet including England’s highest and about 50 lakes and tarns including England’s biggest and deepest; it has connections with leading literary figures including Wordsworth, Coleridge and de Quincey; as England’s only true mountain region, the park covers 885 square miles (33 miles north to south and 40 miles east to west), about 85 percent of the area of Rhode Island.
    • Yorkshire Dales: the Dales are broad and winding U-shaped valleys carved out by glaciers that sometimes narrow near the bottom, producing an effect of wide gentle hills often cut by steeper slopes that finish in rivers, streams and woodlands; the 680 square miles of the park cover part of West Yorkshire and Cumbria across the Pennine Hills in the centre of England, and since its establishment in 1954 it has become one of England’s most popular areas for walking, both for gentle strolls and more strenuous hikes.
    • North York Moors: heather-covered moors, seductively dangerous peat bogs, ancient forests and a dramatic North Sea coast are all part of the park, which is dotted with villages of honey-coloured sandstone and marked with historic abbey ruins, castles and ancient pathways; the ruins of Rievaulx Abbey tower over the landscape, and Whitby Abbey above the seaside town of the same name was the scene of the Synod of Whitby in 664, where early Bishops of the Roman and Celtic churches debated and agreed on the date of Easter — its spookily atmospheric ruins inspired Bram Stoker to bring Dracula and his coffin full of Transylvanian soil ashore for his first landfall in Britain here, while Emily Brontë also used the region for inspiration, setting the fictional Wuthering Heights somewhere in the moors.
    • Peak District: the oldest of Britain’s national parks and perhaps because of that the most characteristic of the features preserved in the parks, its landscapes of hills and the wide shallow valleys known as dales cover 555 miles in the heart of England across Derbyshire, Staffordshire and a small part of South Yorkshire; the area is historically rich, with hundreds of listed national monuments, stately homes, magnificent abbeys and storybook villages.
    • Snowdonia: covers 840 square miles of north-west Wales, with 570 square miles protected for conservation or special scientific interest and 20% legally protected because of its wildlife; the highest peaks are Snowdon (3,560 ft) in the north and Cader Idris (2,929 ft) in the south, and the park is dotted with castles, some only ruins, some magnificent, and at least one converted to a B&B.
    • Pembrokeshire Coast: in south-west Wales, the UK’s only totally coastal national park protects 240 square miles including a remarkable 260 linear miles of the coast; beaches, dramatic cliffs, caves and stone stacks as well as several offshore islands form stunning backdrops for rare native shore birds, marine and coastal mammals as well as visiting Atlantic wildlife of all sorts; and for those interested in history and archaeology there is an Iron Age hill fort at Castell Henlley with reconstructed Iron Age roundhouses, and at Carew Castle an ancient fortress and tidal mill.
    • Brecon Beacons: like all the UK’s national parks it is a mixture of landscapes and habitats — rushing streams, green valleys and mysterious forests — but the overriding characteristic of the park are the Beacons themselves: huge open hills and flat-topped mesas ending in softly eroded but still massive bluffs; the topography makes the Beacons popular for riding and hunts, hand gliding and sheep rearing, and the landscape is dotted with dolmens and standing stones, prehistoric tracks and hints of ancient peoples.
    • Norfolk Broads: the United Kingdom’s largest protected wetlands, 117 square miles of lakes, fens, woodlands and grazing marshes between the medieval city of Norwich and the wide East Anglian beaches of the Norfolk and Suffolk coasts; popular for boating and sailing vacations as well as green holidays, it has 125 miles of lock-free, navigable waterways.
    • Remarkably, this water world, criss-crossed by rivers and channels and dotted with lakes (the “broads”), was man-made in the early Middle Ages: by the 12th century records show that the local population had cleared this area of most of its woodland for building and fuel, and peat cutting started about then but was abandoned by the 14th century because the peat bogs became flooded, much as modern quarries eventually flood; the way the Norfolk Broads came to be was only discovered in the 1950s when scientists realized the banks of the broads were vertical rather than the gradual slopes of natural lakes; working windmills dotted around the Broads drain the dry land for animal grazing and human occupation, as they have done for hundreds of years.
    • Exmoor: 267 square miles including high moorland and 34 miles of coast with the highest sea cliffs in England, coastal forests, secluded beaches and headlands; it has 300 miles of rivers and streams leaping with salmon and trout, the otter population is on the increase, and if you are very, very lucky you may see some along a quiet riverbank.
    • Exmoor ponies (which graze wild but are actually owned by local people) are not the only prehistoric creatures here: they are the nearest breed Britain has to the original European wild horses and are rarer than pandas; red deer have existed on Exmoor since prehistoric times and half the red deer in England live on the moor.
    • Dartmoor: with more than 450 miles of public rights of way in South Devon, it is a walkers’ and cyclists’ paradise and is also rich in prehistory, with some of the best archaeological sites in Western Europe; like Exmoor it has its own ancient breed of native ponies, whose hoof prints archaeologists have found in Bronze Age digs — owned and marked by local farmers, they nevertheless live and graze wild on the moor.
    • Dartmoor has a variety of landscapes — valleys, bog and wetlands, ancient forests, castles and ruins — and because of the mists that sometimes cover the moors it has many myths and eerie legends associated with it; Sir Arthur Conan Doyle’s Sherlock Holmes story The Hound of the Baskervilles was inspired by Dartmoor.
    • While the landscape of Dartmoor has natural dangers of its own, particularly bogs and mires, a man-made hazard adds another frisson for walkers: a large part of the park has been used by the military as a live firing range since the early 1800s, and this area is open to the public when firing is not going on, with its borders marked and red flags during the day and red lamps at night notifying the public when firing is going on.
    • New Forest: there is nothing new about the New Forest — William the Conqueror chose it as his royal hunting ground in 1079 and named it Nova Foresta, and the forest is mentioned in the Domesday Book.
    • Only about half the New Forest National Park is actually a forest; the rest is made up of lowland heath (the largest lowland heath area in Europe), pastureland, villages and shoreline; nevertheless the New Forest has the largest area of natural deciduous woodland in Britain, and what forest there is, is ancient — the Knightwood Oak near Bolderwood is thought to be 500 years old, and an even older common yew tree near St Nicholas Church in Brocklewood could be 1,000 years old; the Norman church, by the way, is the oldest in the New Forest and was where kings prayed when they came to the forest to hunt.
    • New Forest ponies are a recognized breed but, unlike the ponies of Exmoor and Dartmoor, they are not ancient — they originate from Welsh ponies crossbred with Exmoor, Dartmoor and Highland ponies; the ponies have right of way over vehicles on New Forest roads, and five caretakers known as agisters look after the animals in the forest, with the ponies rounded up once a year in “drifts” for health checks, branding and sales.
    • South Downs: one of the UK’s newest national parks (2009), made up of England’s chalk downs stretching from the white cliffs near Eastbourne on the south coast to Winchester; the park follows the 99-mile route of the South Downs Way, a prehistoric footpath across the tops of the Downs, and takes in dozens of historic towns and villages as well as prehistoric formations.
  • Demography and stereotypes
    • The population of the UK is over 68 million, and the shape of Britain’s population in age and composition has been changing substantially, with the balance between the young and the old continuing to shift; by percentage of total population, England accounts for 84.3%, Scotland 8.1%, Wales 4.7% and Northern Ireland 2.8%.
    • Age structure (2023 est.): 0–14 years 16.91% (male 5,901,611 / female 5,620,070); 15–64 years 64.03% (male 21,997,962 / female 21,628,742); 65 years and over 19.06% (male 5,953,187 / female 7,036,912).
    • The birth rate continues to fall: 10.8 births per 1,000 population (2023 est.); death rate 9.12 deaths per 1,000 (2023 est.); mother’s mean age at first birth 29 years (2018 est.); total fertility rate 1.63 children born per woman (2023 est.); population growth rate 0.49% (2023 est.).
    • The British population is already one of the oldest in Europe and is currently getting older: in 1990 the median age was 36 and in 2017 it was 40.5; median age (2020 est.) total 40.6 years, male 39.6, female 41.7; life expectancy at birth (2023 est.) total 82.05 years, male 80.01, female 84.21.
    • Britain is also changing ethnically: there used to be an assumption that the British were nearly all Anglo-Saxon, in spite of substantial immigration from continental Europe during the first half of the century, but since black people from the Caribbean were first recruited to fill job vacancies during the 1950s over 2 million Afro-Caribbean and Asian people have come to live and work in Britain, and during the 1960s and 1970s a large number of people also came from India, Pakistan and Bangladesh.
    • Afro-Caribbeans and Asians experience many kinds of disadvantage: they find greater difficulty getting a job, and immigrants also tend to receive the worst housing.
    • Ethnic groups (2011 est.): white 87.2%, black/African/Caribbean/black British 3%, Asian/Asian British Indian 2.3%, Asian/Asian British Pakistani 1.9%, mixed 2%, other 3.7%.
    • The official language of the UK is English, but the following are recognized regional languages: Scots (about 30% of the population of Scotland), Scottish Gaelic (about 60,000 in Scotland), Welsh (about 20% of the population of Wales), Irish (about 10% of the population of Northern Ireland) and Cornish (some 2,000 to 3,000 in Cornwall) (2012 est.).
    • There are two established or state churches in Britain: the Church of England (the Anglican Church) and the Church of Scotland (the “Kirk”); the free or nonconformist churches are distinguished by having no bishops, the main ones today being the Methodist Union, the Baptists, the United Reformed Church and the Salvation Army.
    • The Roman Catholic Church returned to Britain in 1950, and the Catholic Church in England is composed of four main strands: immigrants from Ireland, working-class people in deprived areas, a few upper-class families, and middle-class converts; all the formal churches are in numerical decline.
    • Apart from Christianity there are at least five other religions in Britain: the oldest is the Jewish community, since Jews returned to England in the 17th century after their previous expulsion in the 13th century, and there are also more recently established religious groups — Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists and Muslims.
    • Religions (2011 est.): Christian (including Anglican, Roman Catholic, Presbyterian, Methodist) 59.5%, Muslim 4.4%, Hindu 1.3%, other 2%, unspecified 7.2%, none 25.7%; net migration rate 3.23 migrants per 1,000 population (2023 est.).
    • Population distribution: the core of the population lies in and around London, with significant clusters found in central Britain around Manchester and Liverpool, in the Scottish lowlands between Edinburgh and Glasgow, in southern Wales in and around Cardiff, and in far eastern Northern Ireland centred on Belfast; the urban population is 83.1% of the total (2017) with a rate of urbanization of 0.82% annual rate of change (2015–20 est.).
    • Major urban areas by population: London (capital) 10.313 million; Manchester 2.646 million; Birmingham 2.515 million; Glasgow 1.223 million; Southampton/Portsmouth 882,000; Liverpool 870,000 (2015).
    • British stereotypes (common stereotypes and misconceptions): (1) all Britons like queuing — queuing is a norm in a range of everyday situations and “queue jumpers” are often frowned upon; (2) all Britons hate other nationalities; (3) all Britons speak Queen’s English; (4) it rains every day in Britain; (5) Britons drink tea all day — each Brit consumes 1.89 kg of tea per year according to 2009 figures, ranking 6th in a list topped by the United Arab Emirates, followed by Ireland and Mauritania, and tea is the 2nd most popular drink in the world next to water; (6) Britons are obsessed with the class system — although traditional class boundaries have become more blurred, most Britons tend to identify with one of the 3 main classes, and evidence suggests that Britain unfortunately remains divided along class lines.
    • (7) Britons love to celebrate Royal occasions — Royal Weddings and Jubilees are lavish occasions which millions love to celebrate by waving Union Jacks, organising street parties and donning fancy dress, and 24.5 million viewers watched the Royal Wedding of Prince William and Princess Catherine on TV; (8) Britons have terrible food — with fish and chips, Sunday dinners and Nando’s it is hard to believe other nations think the UK doesn’t serve up good grub; (9) British people are rude; (10) Britons love pubs; (11) Britons dislike talking to strangers; (12) Britons like saying “Sorry”; (13) Britons are bad at foreign languages; (14) Britons follow the rules; (15) Britons love talking about the weather.
  • London
    • London is the capital and most populous city of England and the United Kingdom; it was founded by the Romans, who named it Londinium, and it is a leading global city in the arts, commerce, education, entertainment, fashion, finance, healthcare, media, professional services, research and development, tourism and transportation.
    • It is the world’s largest financial centre, a world cultural capital, the world’s most-visited city as measured by international arrivals, and has the world’s largest city airport system measured by passenger traffic; London’s universities form the largest concentration of higher education institutes in Europe, and in 2012 London became the first city to have hosted the modern Summer Olympic Games three times.
    • London’s urban area is the second most populous in the EU after Paris, with 9,787,426 inhabitants at the 2011 census; London was the world’s most populous city from around 1831 to 1925.
    • Architecture: London is not characterised by any particular architectural style, having accumulated its buildings over a long period; few structures predate the Great Fire of 1666, with notable exceptions including the Tower of London, Westminster Abbey, Banqueting House and several scattered Tudor survivors in the City of London.
    • The City itself contains a wide variety of styles, progressing through Wren’s late 17th-century churches and the financial institutions of the 18th and 19th centuries (such as the Royal Exchange and the Bank of England) to the early 20th-century Old Bailey and the 1960s Barbican Estate; London has a growing number of skyscrapers, with twelve buildings under construction to rise above 100 metres and a further 44 approved.
    • London contains four World Heritage Sites: the Tower of London; Kew Gardens; the site comprising the Palace of Westminster, Westminster Abbey and St Margaret’s Church; and the historic settlement of Greenwich (in which the Royal Observatory defines the Prime Meridian, 0° longitude, and GMT); other famous landmarks include Buckingham Palace, the London Eye, Piccadilly Circus, St Paul’s Cathedral, Tower Bridge, Trafalgar Square and The Shard.
    • London is home to numerous museums, galleries, libraries, sporting events and other cultural institutions, including the British Museum, the National Gallery, the Natural History Museum, Tate Modern, the British Library and West End theatres.
    • The mayor is Sadiq Khan, the first Muslim mayor of a major Western capital; since 1965 Greater London has been divided into 32 London boroughs in addition to the ancient City of London.
    • Districts: the City of London is the main financial district; the West End is London’s main entertainment and shopping district, attracting tourists; the City of Westminster is an Inner London borough which also holds city status and occupies much of the central area of Greater London including most of the West End; the East End is the area closest to the original Port of London, known for its high immigrant population as well as for being one of the poorest areas in London.
    • Transport: London has an extensive and developed transport network including both private and public services; journeys made by public transport account for 37% of London’s journeys while private services account for 36%; the public transport network serves as the central hub for the United Kingdom in rail, air and road transport.
    • There are several independent airports operating in London, including Heathrow, the busiest airport in the United Kingdom; the London Underground is the oldest underground railway network in the world, having begun operations in 1863, and has 11 lines; the Tramlink is a tram and light rail system in South London.
    • The red double-decker London bus has entered popular culture as an internationally recognised British icon, and while the design and operation of London buses has changed over the years the vehicles still maintain their traditional red colour.
    • The iconic black cab remains a common sight: they are driven by the only taxicab drivers in the world who have spent at least three years learning the city’s road network to gain “The Knowledge”; the London taxicab driver is required to be able to decide routes immediately in response to a passenger’s request or traffic conditions, rather than stopping to look at a map, relying on satellite navigation or asking a controller by radio — consequently the “Knowledge of London” is the in-depth study of a number of pre-set London street routes and all places of interest that taxicab drivers must complete to obtain a licence to operate a black cab; it was initiated in 1865 and has changed little since.
    • The River Thames is generally no more than 300 m wide as it runs through central London and is crossed by many bridges and tunnels; river boat services mostly travel east or west along the Thames rather than across it, and the only major cross-river ferry services are to be found outside London further downstream where the river is wider.
    • London also has several canals, including the Regent’s Canal, which links the Thames to the Grand Union Canal and thus to the waterway network across much of England; these canals were originally built in the Industrial Revolution for the transport of coal, raw materials and foods, and although they now carry few goods they are popular with private narrowboat users and leisure cruisers, and a regular “water bus” service operates along the Regent’s Canal during the summer months.
    • In 2015 the top most-visited attractions in the UK were all in London: the British Museum (6,820,686), the National Gallery (5,908,254), the Natural History Museum, South Kensington (5,284,023), the Southbank Centre (5,102,883), Tate Modern (4,712,581), the Victoria and Albert Museum, South Kensington (3,432,325), the Science Museum (3,356,212), Somerset House (3,235,104), the Tower of London (2,785,249) and the National Portrait Gallery (2,145,486).
    • Parks: the largest parks in the central area of London are three of the eight Royal Parks — Hyde Park and its neighbour Kensington Gardens in the west, and Regent’s Park to the north; Hyde Park in particular is popular for sports and sometimes hosts open-air concerts, while Regent’s Park contains London Zoo, the world’s oldest scientific zoo, and is near the tourist attraction of Madame Tussauds Wax Museum.
    • Primrose Hill, immediately to the north of Regent’s Park, at 256 feet, is a popular spot from which to view the city skyline; close to Hyde Park are the smaller Royal Parks, Green Park and St James’s Park.
    • A number of large parks lie outside the city centre, including Hampstead Heath and the remaining Royal Parks of Greenwich Park to the south-east and Bushy Park and Richmond Park (the largest) to the south-west; Hampton Court Park is also a royal park but, because it contains a palace, it is administered by the Historic Royal Palaces, unlike the eight Royal Parks.
    • Close to Richmond Park is Kew Gardens, which has the world’s largest collection of living plants and was put on the UNESCO list of World Heritage Sites in 2003; there are also numerous parks administered by London’s borough councils, including Victoria Park in the East End and Battersea Park in the centre.
    • Some more informal, semi-natural open spaces also exist, including the 320-hectare Hampstead Heath of North London and Epping Forest, which covers 2,476 hectares in the east — both are controlled by the City of London Corporation; Hampstead Heath incorporates Kenwood House, a former stately home and a popular location in the summer months when classical music concerts are held by the lake, attracting thousands of people every weekend.
  • Edinburgh, Belfast and Cardiff
    • Edinburgh is one of Europe’s most beautiful cities, draped across a series of rocky hills overlooking the sea, and is a town intimately entwined with its landscape, with buildings and monuments perched atop crags and overshadowed by cliffs.
    • From the Old Town’s picturesque jumble of medieval tenements piled high along the Royal Mile — its turreted skyline strung between the black, bull-nosed Castle Rock and the russet palisade of Salisbury Crags — to the New Town’s neat grid of neoclassical respectability, the city offers a constantly changing perspective.
    • Nicknamed the “Athens of the North” by the great thinkers of the Scottish Enlightenment, it is a city of high culture and lofty ideals, of art and literature, philosophy and science; each summer the world’s biggest arts festival rises here, and beneath the Greek temples of Calton Hill — Edinburgh’s acropolis — the Scottish parliament sits again after a 300-year absence.
    • Edinburgh is also known as Auld Reekie, a down-to-earth place that flicks an impudent finger at the pretensions of the literati: a city of loud, crowded pubs and decadent restaurants, late-night drinking and all-night parties, beer-fuelled poets and foul-mouthed comedians; it is the city that tempted Robert Louis Stevenson from his law lectures to explore the drinking dens and lurid street life of the 19th-century Old Town, and the city of Beltane, the resurrected pagan May Day festival.
    • Edinburgh is Scotland’s second most populous city and the seventh most populous in the United Kingdom; it is famous for the Edinburgh International Festival and the Fringe, the latter being the world’s largest annual international arts festival.
    • Historic sites include Edinburgh Castle, Holyrood Palace, the churches of St Giles, Greyfriars and the Canongate, and the extensive Georgian New Town built in the 18th century; Edinburgh’s Old Town and New Town together are listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, managed by Edinburgh World Heritage since 1999.
    • The city comprises distinct areas that retain much of their original character as settlements in existence before they were absorbed into the sprawling city of the nineteenth century; many areas, such as Dalry, contain residences that are multi-occupancy buildings known as tenements, although the more southern and western parts of the city have traditionally been more affluent with a greater number of detached and semi-detached villas.
    • The historic centre of Edinburgh is divided in two by the broad green swathe of Princes Street Gardens: to the south the view is dominated by Edinburgh Castle, built high on the castle rock, and the long sweep of the Old Town descending towards Holyrood Palace, while to the north lie Princes Street and the New Town; the West End includes the financial district, with insurance and banking offices as well as the Edinburgh International Conference Centre.
    • Edinburgh’s Old and New Towns were listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1995 in recognition of the unique character of the Old Town with its medieval street layout and the planned Georgian New Town, including the adjoining Dean Village and Calton Hill areas; the Southside is a popular residential part of the city.
    • Leith was historically the port of Edinburgh, an arrangement of unknown date that was reconfirmed by the royal charter Robert the Bruce granted to the city in 1329; the port developed a separate identity from Edinburgh, which to some extent it still retains, and it was a matter of great resentment when the two burghs merged in 1920 into the City of Edinburgh — even today the parliamentary seat is known as “Edinburgh North and Leith”; the loss of traditional industries and commerce (the last shipyard closed in 1983) resulted in economic decline.
    • The coastal suburb of Portobello is characterised by Georgian villas, Victorian tenements, a popular beach and promenade, and cafés, bars, restaurants and independent shops, with rowing and sailing clubs and a restored Victorian swimming pool including Turkish baths.
    • Edinburgh has many museums and libraries, including the National Museum of Scotland, the National Library of Scotland, the National War Museum, the Museum of Edinburgh, Surgeons’ Hall Museum, the Writers’ Museum, the Museum of Childhood and Our Dynamic Earth, while the Museum on the Mound has exhibits on money and banking.
    • Edinburgh Zoo, covering 82 acres on Corstorphine Hill, is the second most popular paid tourist attraction in Scotland; it was the first zoo in the world to house and to breed penguins, and it is also the only zoo in Britain to house koalas and giant pandas.
    • Edinburgh Airport is located 8 miles to the west of the city centre and is the principal international gateway to the city as well as the busiest airport in Scotland; bus transportation is the principal means of public transport, with an extensive bus network covering all parts of the city, its suburbs and the surrounding city-region, widely regarded as one of the most extensive bus services in the country.
    • The city currently has 12 railway stations within the City of Edinburgh Council area; the Edinburgh Trams is a new tram line which opened on 31 May 2014; the Union Canal was opened in 1822 and provides an inland waterway link from Edinburgh to Falkirk and from there to Glasgow via the Forth and Clyde Canal — it is currently owned by the public corporation Scottish Canals and chiefly used for leisure purposes.
    • Belfast is traditionally divided into four main areas — North Belfast, East Belfast, South Belfast and West Belfast — which meet at Belfast City Centre; the second traditional divide is formed by the River Lagan, with the northern bank of the river being part of County Antrim while the southern bank is part of County Down.
    • The Cathedral Quarter takes its name from St Anne’s Cathedral (Church of Ireland) and has taken on the mantle of the city’s key cultural locality, hosting a yearly visual and performing arts festival; Custom House Square is one of the city’s main outdoor venues for free concerts and street entertainment.
    • The Gaeltacht Quarter is an area around the Falls Road in west Belfast which promotes and encourages the use of the Irish language; the Queen’s Quarter in south Belfast is named after Queen’s University, has a large student population and hosts the annual Belfast International Arts Festival each autumn, and is home to Botanic Gardens and the Ulster Museum, which was reopened in 2009 after major redevelopment.
    • The Golden Mile is the name given to the mile between Belfast City Hall and Queen’s University, taking in Dublin Road, Great Victoria Street, Shaftesbury Square and Bradbury Place, and containing some of the best bars and restaurants in the city; since the Good Friday Agreement in 1998 the nearby Lisburn Road has developed into the city’s most exclusive shopping strip.
    • Finally, the Titanic Quarter covers 0.75 km² of reclaimed land adjacent to Belfast Harbour, formerly known as Queen’s Island; named after RMS Titanic, which was built here in 1912, work has begun which promises to transform some former shipyard land into “one of the largest waterfront developments in Europe”, with plans including apartments, a riverside entertainment district and a major Titanic-themed museum.
    • Architecture: the architecture of Belfast comprises many styles ranging from Georgian through to state-of-the-art modern buildings like the Waterfront Hall and Titanic Belfast; the city’s beautiful Victorian and Edwardian buildings are notable for their display of a large number of sculptures, and many of Belfast’s Victorian landmarks, including the main Lanyon Building at Queen’s University in 1849, were designed by Sir Charles Lanyon.
    • The City Hall was finished in 1906 and was built to reflect Belfast’s city status, granted by Queen Victoria in 1888; its dome is 53 m high; among the city’s grandest buildings are two former banks, the Ulster Bank in Waring Street (built in 1860) and the Northern Bank in nearby Donegall Street (built in 1769); the Royal Courts of Justice in Chichester Street are home to Northern Ireland’s Supreme Court.
    • Many of Belfast’s oldest buildings are found in the Cathedral Quarter area, which is currently undergoing redevelopment as the city’s main cultural and tourist area; Windsor House, 80 m high with 23 floors, is the second tallest building in Ireland.
    • The ornately decorated Crown Liquor Saloon, designed by Joseph Anderson in 1876 in Great Victoria Street, is one of only two pubs in the UK that are owned by the National Trust (the other is the George Inn, Southwark in London); it was made internationally famous as the setting for the classic film Odd Man Out, starring James Mason, and the restaurant panels in the Crown Bar were originally made for the Britannic, the sister ship of the Titanic, built in Belfast.
    • The Harland and Wolff shipyard has two of the largest dry docks in Europe, where the giant cranes Samson and Goliath stand out against Belfast’s skyline; including the Waterfront Hall and the Odyssey Arena, Belfast has several other venues for performing arts.
    • The architecture of the Grand Opera House has an oriental theme and was completed in 1895; it was bombed several times during the Troubles but has now been restored to its former glory; the Lyric Theatre, re-opened on 1 May 2011 after a rebuilding programme, is the only full-time producing theatre in the country and is where film star Liam Neeson began his career; the Ulster Hall (1859–1862) was originally designed for grand dances but is now used primarily as a concert and sporting venue — Lloyd George, Parnell and Patrick Pearse all attended political rallies there.
    • Parks: from the Victorian Botanic Gardens in the heart of the city to the heights of Cave Hill Country Park and the great expanse of Lagan Valley Regional Park to Colin Glen, Belfast contains an abundance of parkland and forest parks; parks and gardens are an integral part of Belfast’s heritage and home to an abundance of local wildlife and popular places for a picnic, a stroll or a jog, with numerous events throughout the year including festivals such as Rose Week and special activities such as bird watching evenings.
    • Belfast has over forty public parks; with an average of 670,000 visitors per year between 2007 and 2011, one of the most popular is Botanic Gardens in the Queen’s Quarter — built in the 1830s and designed by Sir Charles Lanyon, the Botanic Gardens Palm House is one of the earliest examples of a curvilinear and cast iron glasshouse, and other attractions include the Tropical Ravine, a humid jungle glen built in 1889, rose gardens and public events ranging from live opera broadcasts to pop concerts (U2 played here in 1997).
    • Sir Thomas and Lady Dixon Park, to the south of the city centre, attracts thousands of visitors each year to its International Rose Garden, and Rose Week in July each year features over 20,000 blooms; it has an area of 128 acres of meadows, woodland and gardens and features a Diana, Princess of Wales Memorial Garden, a Japanese garden, a walled garden, and the Golden Crown Fountain commissioned in 2002 as part of the Queen’s Golden Jubilee celebrations.
    • Belfast Zoo is owned by Belfast City Council, which spends £1.5 million every year on running and promoting the zoo — one of the few local government-funded zoos in the UK and Ireland; it is one of the top visitor attractions in Northern Ireland, receiving more than 295,000 visitors a year, and the majority of the animals are in danger in their natural habitat.
    • The zoo houses more than 1,200 animals of 140 species including Asian elephants, Barbary lions, Malayan sun bears (one of the few in the United Kingdom), two species of penguin, a family of western lowland gorillas, a troop of common chimpanzees, a pair of red pandas, a pair of Goodfellow’s tree-kangaroos and Francois’ langurs; the zoo also carries out important conservation work and takes part in European and international breeding programmes.
    • Transportation systems in Belfast include road, air, rail and sea; it is still a relatively car-dependent city, but it is also served by a comprehensive rail and bus network; Belfast also ran electric trams prior to 1954, the city has two major airports, and the Port of Belfast is the busiest ferry port on the island of Ireland; Belfast is one of the most visited cities in the UK and the second most visited on the island of Ireland.
    • Titanic Belfast is a major visitor attraction and a monument to Belfast’s maritime heritage on the site of the former Harland & Wolff shipyard, opened on 31 March 2012; it features a criss-cross of escalators and suspended walkways and nine high-tech galleries.
    • The Albert Memorial Clock was erected in 1865 in Gothic style to commemorate Queen Victoria’s consort, Prince Albert; it was built on reclaimed land from the River Lagan on wooden piles, causing the characteristic list; at 113 ft it was tall enough to offer an excellent vantage point for at least one enterprising sightseer to get a bird’s-eye view of Titanic’s launch, and it features a statue of Prince Albert as well as ornately carved crowned lions and floral decorations and a 2-tonne bell inside.
    • Cave Hill has for generations been synonymous with Belfast, with its imposing outline visible throughout the city; the landmark, named for the five caves located on the side of the cliffs, contains a wealth of natural, archaeological and historical features including Belfast Castle; its most famous feature, known locally as Napoleon’s Nose, is believed to have been the inspiration for Jonathan Swift’s novel Gulliver’s Travels.
    • The park is home to the Cave Hill Adventurous Playground, archaeological sites, a Visitor Information Area in Belfast Castle, ecotrails, and walking and orienteering routes; the estate contains landscaped gardens and mature mixed woodland and offers superb views of the city from a variety of vantage points, and it is home to many different species of wildlife, including long-eared owls, sparrowhawks and Belfast’s rarest plant, the town hall clock.
    • Belfast Cathedral is the church of Saint Anne; it seeks to maintain an Anglican and Irish ethos and identity in its daily worship whilst providing a hospitable space for civic, community and inter-church services, and a living partnership is maintained with St Peter’s Roman Catholic Cathedral in the city.
    • The building itself is Romanesque, giving it a lofty grandeur associated with that style: semi-circular arches and massive pillars, vast and high single windows, and an uncluttered spaciousness; the Cathedral contains mosaics designed by Sir Charles Nicholson as well as sculptures by Rosamund Praegar and Maurice Harding, and the 40-metre stainless steel Spire of Hope was added in 2007 and is illuminated at night.
    • Cardiff is the capital and largest city in Wales and the eleventh-largest city in the United Kingdom; it is the country’s chief commercial centre, the base for most national cultural and sporting institutions, the Welsh national media, and the seat of the National Assembly for Wales.
    • Cardiff was made a city in 1905 and proclaimed the capital of Wales in 1955; since the 1980s it has seen significant development, and a new waterfront area at Cardiff Bay contains the Senedd building, home to the Welsh Assembly, and the Wales Millennium Centre arts complex.
    • Transport in Cardiff involves road, rail, bus, water and air; domestic and international air links to Cardiff and South & West Wales are provided from Cardiff Airport (CWL), the only international airport in Wales; the city has an urban rail metro network operated by Arriva Trains Wales known as the Valley Lines, with Cardiff Central and Queen Street as the hubs connecting Cardiff’s northern, southern and western suburbs to the city centre.
    • Cardiff has many landmark buildings such as the Principality Stadium, the Pierhead Building, the Welsh National Museum and the Senedd, the home of the National Assembly for Wales; it is also famous for Cardiff Castle, St David’s Hall, Llandaff Cathedral and the Wales Millennium Centre.
    • Cardiff Castle is a major tourist attraction situated in the heart of the city centre; the National History Museum at St Fagans is a large open-air museum housing dozens of buildings from throughout Welsh history that have been moved to the site.
    • The Civic Centre in Cathays Park comprises a collection of Edwardian buildings such as the City Hall, the National Museum and Gallery of Wales, Cardiff Crown Court and buildings forming part of Cardiff University, together with more modern civic buildings; these buildings are laid out around the Queen Alexandra Gardens, a formal park which contains the Welsh National War Memorial and a number of other smaller memorials.
    • In addition to Cardiff Castle, Castell Coch (the Red Castle) is located in Tongwynlais in the north of the city; the current castle is an elaborately decorated Victorian folly designed by William Burges for the Marquess and built in the 1870s as an occasional retreat, but it stands on the footings of a much older medieval castle possibly built by Ifor Bach, a regional baron with links to Cardiff Castle also; the exterior has become a popular location for film and television productions.
    • Cardiff claims to have the largest concentration of castles of any city in the world: as well as Cardiff Castle and Castell Coch, there are the remains of two Motte and Bailey castles in Radyr and Rhiwbina, which along with a castle at Whitchurch (known as Treoda and destroyed by housing in the 1960s) formed an arc of fortifications which divided the Norman lordship from the Welsh lordship of Senghenydd.
    • Further up the Cefn Cibwr ridge on the boundary with Caerphilly there is also another ruined castle, known as Castell Morgraig; archaeological evidence suggests this castle was never finished, and it is debated whether the fortification was of Norman or Welsh origin; the concentration of these castles is indicative of the moveable nature of the border between the Norman lordship of Glamorgan, centred at Cardiff, and its Welsh neighbours to the north.
    • While not strictly castles in the historic sense, there is also the ruined Llandaff Bishop’s Palace, a fortified residence belonging to the Bishops of Llandaff, and Saint Fagans Castle, a preserved seventeenth-century manor house once the seat of the Earls of Plymouth.
    • Other major tourist attractions are the Cardiff Bay regeneration sites, which include the recently opened Wales Millennium Centre and the Senedd, and many other cultural sites of interest including the Cardiff Bay Barrage and the famous Coal Exchange; the New Theatre was founded in 1906 and completely refurbished in the 1980s, and until the opening of the Wales Millennium Centre in 2004 it was the premier venue in Wales for touring theatre and dance companies.
    • Other venues popular for concerts and sporting events include the Motorpoint Arena, St David’s Hall and the Principality Stadium; the Cardiff Story, a museum documenting the city’s history, has been open to the public since spring 2011.
    • Cardiff has over 1,000 listed buildings, ranging from the more prominent buildings such as the castles to smaller buildings, houses and structures; Cathedral Road was developed by the 3rd Marquis of Bute and is lined by fine villas, some backing on to Sophia Gardens.
    • Cardiff has walks of special interest for tourists and ramblers alike, such as the Centenary Walk, which runs for 2.3 miles within Cardiff city centre and passes through many of Cardiff’s landmarks and historic buildings; the city is known for its extensive parkland, with parks and other green spaces covering around 10% of the city’s total area.

Seminar 9 — The Political System of the United Kingdom

Central concept: the party system, local government and trade unions in the UK.

  • Conservative Party
    • The Conservative Party, officially the Conservative and Unionist Party, is one of the leading parties and the main centre-right political party in the United Kingdom; its policies usually promote conservatism and British Unionism.
    • The current leader is Kemi Badenoch, who was elected to the position on 2 November 2024 following an unopposed victory in the party’s leadership election.
    • The current party was first organized in the 1830s; one of the most important things it accepted from the Whigs is the teaching of John Locke about government and about property.
    • One of the main concepts of the Conservatives is that the State must protect property, and that widely distributed private property is the best defence against totalitarianism.
    • The Conservative Party has no official permanent programme: before a general election the party issues a pre-election manifesto which states the main aspects of the home and foreign policies of the future Conservative government if the party wins the election.
    • The members of the Conservative Party come from different groups: among them are the country aristocracy (consisting of big landowners), smaller farmers and businessmen in small towns and cities, and there are also many working-class people who vote for Conservative candidates because they believe in social reform but not in socialism.
  • Labour Party
    • The Labour Party was established in 1900 on the initiative of the trade unions and several social organizations; the main aim was to win working-class representation in Parliament, which was initially reflected in the name of the party — the Labour Representation Committee.
    • In 1906 the Committee officially adopted the title of the Labour Party; after the First World War it proclaimed its socialist ideas, with a programme calling for nationalization and equality of wealth; today the Labour Party advocates a mixed programme based on the platform of social-democratic reformism.
    • The Labour Party is a centre-left political party in the United Kingdom; Keir Starmer is the leader of the party.
    • Membership of the Labour Party is also mixed: it is a membership organisation consisting of individual members and Constituency Labour Parties, affiliated trade unions, socialist societies and the Co-operative Party, with which it has an electoral agreement.
    • Members who are elected to parliamentary positions take part in the Parliamentary Labour Party (PLP) and the European Parliamentary Labour Party (EPLP).
    • The party’s decision-making bodies on a national level formally include the National Executive Committee (NEC), the Labour Party Conference and the National Policy Forum (NPF) — although in practice the Parliamentary leadership has the final say on policy.
  • Scottish National Party (SNP)
    • The Scottish National Party is a nationalist political party that has sought to make Scotland an independent state within the European Union.
    • The SNP was formed in 1934 from a union of the National Party of Scotland (founded in 1928) and the Scottish Party (1932).
    • From the beginning, disagreements arose within the SNP over whether to focus on electoral politics or wider cultural aims, and there were also deep divisions over its relations with other political parties, primarily the Labour Party.
    • World War II created further controversy concerning the British government’s right to declare war on behalf of the Scottish people.
    • After 1945 the SNP helped to circulate petitions demanding a Scottish Parliament, but until the 1960s the movement for Scottish independence achieved little success and was sustained only by symbolic and insubstantial victories, such as the theft of the Stone of Scone — the ancient stone upon which Scottish kings were crowned — from Westminster Abbey in 1950; in the meantime the SNP became more pragmatic and disciplined, though the party was still plagued by internal divisions and inexperienced leadership.
    • The SNP’s greatest success came in the general election of October 1974: managing for once to mobilize a sense of economic grievance and cultural resentment that cut across the traditional class divisions of Scottish politics, the party won more than 30 percent of the Scottish vote and took 11 of the 71 Scottish seats in the British Parliament.
    • In later years the party was supported consistently in opinion polls by 20 to 25 percent of Scottish voters, making it the second most popular party in Scotland after Labour; the current Scottish National Party leader is John Swinney.
    • The SNP accepts devolution — that is, the legal decentralization of power to the British regions — as one stage on the road to independence for Scotland; overall the party describes itself as moderate and left of centre, tends to be staunchly anti-Conservative in its party politics and interventionist in its economic policies, and is committed to a non-nuclear defence.
  • Liberal Democrats and UKIP
    • The Liberal Democrats is a British political party founded in 1988 through a merger of the Liberal Party and the Social Democratic Party (SDP), which was formed as a result of the split in the Labour Party in 1981.
    • In the middle ground between the dominant Labour Party and the Conservative Party, the Liberal Democrats occupy a centre-left, libertarian position; Sir Ed Davey is the leader of the party, having stood in the 2020 leadership election where he defeated Layla Moran to become permanent Leader of the Liberal Democrats with 63.5% of the vote.
    • The Liberal Democrats have maintained the old Liberal Party’s tradition of radical or social liberalism; in particular they have laid great emphasis on issues of constitutional reform, including electoral reform, devolution of state authority from the centre to the regions, reform of the House of Lords, and the need for freedom-of-information legislation and a bill of rights.
    • The Liberal Democrats take a left-of-centre stance on educational and social issues and are committed to European integration; they are a federal party of the parties of England, Scotland and Wales, with the English and Scottish parties further split into regions, and the parliamentary parties of the House of Commons, the House of Lords, the Scottish Parliament and the Welsh Assembly form semi-autonomous units within the party.
    • The UK Independence Party has, as its name implies, one key policy — to leave the European Union; it is a simple, understandable message which has led to the party gaining bigger and bigger support in European elections.
    • It has spent considerable effort on broadening its appeal, spelling out how leaving the EU is the answer to a whole range of issues, notably controlling immigration, while also outlining plans to cut taxes for middle earners, speaking up for grammar schools and opposing gay marriage.
    • The party has its roots in the Anti-Federalist League, a group led by London School of Economics professor Alan Sked that campaigned against the 1991 Maastricht Treaty on European Union; Sked founded UKIP in 1993, following Britain’s ratification of the Maastricht Treaty, the document that established the European Union.
    • The party is currently led in the interim by Neil Hamilton, with former acting leader Pat Mountain as Deputy Leader; ideologically positioned on the right wing of British politics, political scientists characterise UKIP as part of a broader European radical right.
    • Governed by its leader and National Executive Committee, UKIP is divided into twelve regional groups, with an additional one representing Gibraltar; as the party’s name suggests, UKIP has always had the politics of national identity at its core — the party is nationalist in orientation, and its basic claim, “that the highest priority for the British polity is to assure that it is fully governed by the national state”, is a nationalist one.
  • Local government: county and district councils
    • Administratively the UK is divided into counties, and the county council is the most important unit of local government; its responsibilities include industrial planning, local roads, transport, the police, the fire service, education, social care, libraries, waste management and trading standards.
    • The full council (a meeting of all council members) is responsible for all decisions, but in practice most of the work is given to smaller groups of councillors or council officers (paid staff).
    • Every council must publish details of when key decisions will be taken, papers of meetings at least 5 working days beforehand, and minutes of meetings showing the decisions that were made.
    • Many councils have a civic mayor or chairman of the council: they carry out ceremonial duties and chair meetings but can’t make decisions about council business; some councils have an elected mayor, who is responsible for the day-to-day running of local services; councils can have both elected and civic mayors.
    • Local councillors are elected for 4-year terms by the local community to represent its views; elections to councils are normally held on the first Thursday in May; some councils elect all of their councillors at the same time while other councils elect half or a third of their councillors at each election; all local councillors have to declare any interests, gifts or hospitality they get that could influence decisions they make.
    • Each county is divided into districts of between 60 and 100 thousand people, and the district councils are responsible for housing, rubbish collection, recycling, Council Tax collections, planning applications and other minor things.
    • District councils as well as county councils are run by part-time unpaid councillors who are elected the same way as MPs; the councillors appoint full-time specialists who run different departments; some districts, especially in big cities, have the name of boroughs.
    • There are a total of 326 districts made up of 36 metropolitan boroughs, 32 London boroughs, 201 non-metropolitan districts, 55 unitary authorities, as well as the City of London and the Isles of Scilly, which are also districts but do not correspond to any of these categories.
  • Trade unions in the UK
    • In nearly all industries workers are organized in trade unions; the total membership of British trade unions is about 7.5 million people, and the national centre for the trade union movement is the TUC (Trades Union Congress), which was founded in 1868.
    • Around 60% of trade unionists in the TUC are in the three largest unions, which have grown through mergers; the TUC does not operate in Northern Ireland, and unions operating in both Britain and Northern Ireland are frequently also affiliated to the Irish trade union confederation, the ICTU, through the Northern Ireland Committee of the ICTU — in total the ICTU has 218,514 members in Northern Ireland.
    • British unions are organised in a variety of ways: some organise particular occupations such as teachers or radiographers, and, particularly in the finance industry, some just organise in a single company.
    • The largest union in the UK is Unite, formed in May 2007 through the merger of the previously second and third largest unions, Amicus and the T&G; they work in almost every sector of the economy, including motor manufacturing, printing, finance, road transport and the health service — it is stronger in the private than the public sector but has at least 200,000 members in public services.
    • UNISON, the second largest union with 1.3 million members, organises primarily in the public services, although as a result of privatisation it has substantial membership in private companies; the third largest union is the GMB with 620 thousand — like Unite it is a general union with members in a number of industries, although they are more likely to be manual workers.
    • These three unions account for 56% of total TUC membership, and the two largest unions on their own account for 46%.
    • Around half the membership of the TUC belongs to unions that are affiliated to the Labour Party, although the TUC itself is not affiliated; the exceptions are largely among unions representing professional staff, such as teachers and civil servants.
    • Affiliated unions are present and vote at Party conferences and are represented on the executive committee of the Labour Party, as well as being the largest source of Labour Party funds, accounting for more than 60% of its income — however, as a result of rule changes, unions now have less formal influence on Party policy than in the past.

Seminar 10 — Economy of the United Kingdom

Central concept: the UK as a high-income, diversified non-EU European economy with strong services, deep regional inequality and a complex energy mix.

  • Economic overview and key indicators

  • The UK economy in brief
    • The UK, a leading trading power and financial centre, is the third largest economy in Europe after Germany and France; its economy comprises (in descending order of size) the economies of England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.
    • The UK has been the fastest growing economy in the G7 for 3 consecutive years as of October 2015 and is one of the world’s most globalised economies; services, particularly banking, insurance and business services, are key drivers of British GDP growth, while manufacturing has declined in importance but still accounts for about 10% of economic output.
    • In 2008 the global financial crisis hit the economy particularly hard due to the importance of its financial sector: falling home prices, high consumer debt and the global economic slowdown compounded Britain’s economic problems, pushing the economy into recession in the latter half of 2008 and prompting the then Brown (Labour) government to implement a number of measures to stimulate the economy and stabilize the financial markets.
    • Facing burgeoning public deficits and debt levels, in 2010 the Cameron-led coalition government (between Conservatives and Liberal Democrats) initiated an austerity programme which has continued under the new Conservative majority government; however the deficit still remains one of the highest in the G7 — London intends to eliminate its deficit by 2020, primarily through additional cuts to public spending and welfare benefits, and has also pledged to lower corporation tax from 20% to 18% by 2020.
    • In 2012 weak consumer spending and subdued business investment weighed on the economy, but in 2013 GDP grew 1.7% and in 2014 2.8%, accelerating because of greater consumer spending and a recovering housing market.
    • The service sector dominates the UK economy, contributing around 78% of GDP; the financial services industry is particularly important and London is the world’s largest financial centre (tied with New York); the British aerospace industry is the second- or third-largest national aerospace industry depending on the method of measurement; the pharmaceutical industry plays an important role and the UK has the third-highest share of global pharmaceutical R&D; the automotive industry is also a major employer and exporter.
    • The British economy is boosted by North Sea oil and gas production, with reserves valued at an estimated £120 billion in 2011; there are significant regional variations in prosperity, with the South East of England and southern Scotland the richest areas per capita, and the size of London’s economy makes it the largest city by GDP in Europe.
    • In 2008 the UK entered a recession during the financial crisis of 2007–08, its first for nearly two decades, and initially experienced a deeper downturn than all of the G7 except Japan; however since 2013 the UK has been in a nascent economic recovery, and the economy is now 6.6% bigger than its pre-crisis peak and 13.5% bigger than its lowest point in 2009.
    • The UK was the fastest growing economy out of the G7 members in 2013, 2014 and 2015, and as of 2016 it was suggested that the UK would continue to be the fastest growing economy out of the G7 members; since 2010 the Government has been pursuing an austerity programme aimed at cutting the budget deficit — in the financial year 2009–2010 this was 11% of GDP, and it was 3.9% in 2015.
    • The Bank of England (BoE) is the UK’s central bank and its Monetary Policy Committee is responsible for setting interest rates; the currency of the UK is the pound sterling, which is also the world’s third-largest reserve currency after the US dollar and the euro, and the fourth-most-valued currency in the world behind the Kuwaiti Dinar, Bahraini Dinar and Omani Rial, and the most valued currency outside the Middle East.
  • Regional disparity: the North–South divide
    • The combined effects of changing economic fortunes, economic restructuring and the decline of the UK as an imperial power have created the so-called North–South divide, in which the decaying industrial areas of the north of England and Scotland contrast with the wealthy, finance-and-technology-led southern economy; this has led successive governments to develop regional policies to try and rectify the imbalance.
    • The success has been, at best, patchy, and the uneven distribution of economic wealth in the UK has led to migration from north to south, aggravating serious pressure on the southern housing market.
    • The north–south divide is a long-standing and highly entrenched feature of the UK’s economic landscape, going back to the nineteenth century; since the 1970s the gap has in fact progressively widened — between 1971 and 2013 the south (including London) recorded a 30 percentage point cumulative growth lead over the north.
    • Meanwhile the divide is expected to widen further, with economic output in the capital increasing by an average of 3% a year between 2015 and 2018, while the north-east will see an annual expansion of just 1.6%; only London, the south-east and the east are expected to grow faster than the UK average.
    • Average GVA (gross value added) per head was £42,666 in London, while in Wales it was just £17,573; the fastest growth in GVA per head in 2014 was recorded in London at 5.3%, more than 1 percentage point ahead of the next two regions, Scotland and the east of England, which experienced growth of 4.2%.
    • GVA is a productivity metric that measures the difference between output and intermediate consumption, providing a dollar value for the amount of goods and services produced, less the cost of all inputs and raw materials directly attributable to that production.
    • An average worker in the north would, in effect, have to work more than 24 additional 35-hour weeks each year in order to generate as much economic growth as an average worker in London.
    • The employment rate in the northern region is below the national average, and underemployment (a measure of people who would like to work more hours) is higher; hourly pay also tends to be lower — median hourly earnings in London are more than £20 for men and more than £17 for women, against UK averages of £16 and £14 respectively.
    • Economic growth 2009–2014 was 2.2% in the northern England regions against 7.5% in the southern regions, while in 2013–2014 the figures were 2.7% and 3.5%; employment growth in the UK as a whole was 4.8% a year on average from 2009 to 2014, and 3.1% between 2013 and 2014.
    • London has been the jobs motor of the economy since the recession, with employment up more than 14%; growth in the southern regions outside London is still higher than that seen in the north over this period (4.2%).
    • Away from London, the UK city with the next fastest increase in employment since 2009 has been Birmingham, with Bristol third on the list; Cardiff, Sheffield and Liverpool have all seen employment grow by less than 2%, but the weakest performance has been in Glasgow, where there were still fewer jobs in 2014 than in 2009.
    • One possible cause of the weaker employment growth in the northern regions is that they remain far more reliant on manufacturing, where output is still below the levels reached before the economy started its descent into recession in early 2008; manufacturing is the second biggest source of employment, after health, in the north, but only the seventh biggest in the south.
    • The north of England is “indeed a powerhouse”: its economy is worth £289bn — making it more than twice as big as the Scottish economy — and its cities are growing faster than anywhere else in the UK outside London; it is home to 1 million businesses and 29 universities.
    • Children born into the poorest families in the north of England are falling behind comparable children in the south before they even reach school: less than half of children from the most deprived households in the north reach a good standard of development by that age, and with only 47% of the poorest children in the north meeting that level they lag far behind those in London, where 59% make the standard — only those in the east Midlands, at 43%, fare worse.
    • The north has some of the lowest levels of productivity in the whole of Europe, too much of its labour is under-utilised, and the low incomes that result lead to high levels of poverty and disadvantage; the north–south divide is very far from being history, and closing it remains a highly challenging task.
  • Primary industry, manufacturing and finance
    • The UK’s primary industry sector was once dominated by the coal industry, heavily concentrated in south Wales, the Midlands, Yorkshire, North East England and southern Scotland; the number of pits and miners has been slashed, and output fell by more than 75% between 1981 and 2003.
    • The major primary industry is North Sea oil, with activity concentrated on the east coast of Scotland and North East England; the waters in the North Sea off the east coast of Scotland contain nearly half of the UK’s remaining oil reserves, and a quarter of the reserves are located in the North Sea near the Shetland Islands.
    • A closely related industry is natural gas, which since the 1970s has supplied all of the UK’s gas needs, replacing poisonous coal or town gas; most natural gas production is in the North Sea, with a small amount onshore and in the Irish Sea.
    • A map of the major UK cities gives a good picture of where manufacturing flourished, and often specialisations can be identified: Birmingham (automotive), Glasgow (shipbuilding), London (everything), Manchester (textiles), Newcastle (shipbuilding and steel), Nottingham (apparel, medicine), Sheffield (steel and steel products), Leeds (textiles and engineering) and Belfast (shipbuilding and textiles).
    • Today there is no heavy manufacturing industry in which UK-based firms can be considered world leaders, and no product in which a UK city or region is the world leader; more recently, high-technology firms have concentrated largely along the M4 motorway, partly because of access to Heathrow Airport but also because of agglomeration economies — but the general pattern remains that the south has a lower, and falling, reliance on manufacturing.
    • Finance and services: London is a major centre for international business and commerce and is one of the three “command centres” of the global economy (alongside New York City and Tokyo); there are over 500 banks with offices in London, and it is the leading international centre for banking, insurance, Eurobonds, foreign exchange trading and energy futures.
    • Several other major UK cities have large financial sectors and related services: Edinburgh has one of the largest financial centres in Europe and is home to the headquarters of the Royal Bank of Scotland Group and Standard Life; Leeds is the UK’s largest centre for business and financial services outside London, and the largest centre for legal services in the UK after London.
  • Agriculture
    • The UK has rarely been self-sufficient in terms of food supply; most UK agriculture is intensive and highly mechanised, with the use of chemical fertilisers and insecticides routine; by European standards it is very efficient, although that does not necessarily make it profitable.
    • This intense nature was compounded in the post-war years, with fields being expanded at the expense of hedgerows — a process that has been heavily criticised for damaging biodiversity.
    • Agriculture provides around 60% of Britain’s food needs even though it employs just 1.4% of the country’s labour force; farmers are under pressure to adopt more environmentally friendly methods such as organic farming, which does not use artificial chemicals that can damage the environment and human health and whose popularity has grown rapidly in recent years.
    • Some parts of Britain have excellent soil for crops, while others are used for cattle, sheep, pigs and poultry: in the south-east of England and the lowlands of Scotland grain, potatoes and sugar beet are grown; in the east of England (East Anglia) wheat, barley and vegetables grow in enormous fields.
    • The county of Kent was so well known for this that it is often referred to as the Garden of England and was particularly noted for hop growing; in the south-west of England the rich grass is ideal for feeding dairy cows; in the north-west of England, Wales and Scotland farmers keep cattle and sheep, and sheep can survive the cold winters on the hills and moors.
    • There are many types of farming in Britain: arable (the growing of crops and cereals — the UK is the fourth largest producer of cereal and oilseed crops in the EU after France, Germany and Poland, accounting for about 8% of total EU production); pastoral (rearing and production of animals including pigs, chickens, hill farming sheep, beef and dairy cattle); mixed farming (a combination of arable and pastoral); horticulture (production of flowers, fruit, vegetables or ornamental plants); market gardening (production of fruit and vegetables); and viticulture (grapes).
    • Principal crops: wheat (the most widely grown arable crop in the UK), barley, oats, potatoes, sugar beet (the UK is the fifth largest producer), vegetables, oilseed rape and fruits; livestock products: poultry, sheep, cattle, milk, meat, eggs and wool.
